How to Pay Yourself as a Freelancer: Owner's Draw Explained

By Freelance Ledger · Published November 18, 2026 · How we research

Rules checked on November 18, 2026 against IRS sources: Publication 334 (Tax Guide for Small Business), Publication 583 (Starting a Business and Keeping Records), the single-member LLC page, S corporation compensation page, self-employment tax page, Topic 751 (2026 wage base), estimated taxes page and estimated tax FAQ. Federal rules only; general information, not tax, legal or financial advice.

Short answer: if you freelance as a sole proprietor, or through a single-member LLC taxed the default way, you don’t put yourself on payroll. You move money from your business account to your personal account whenever you like. That transfer is an owner’s draw. It is not a business expense, nothing is withheld from it, and it doesn’t change your tax bill: you are taxed on the business’s profit for the year, however much of it you take out. The practical questions are how much to take, how often, and how much to leave behind for taxes. This guide answers those with a worked example.

What the IRS says

  • You are not your own employee. Publication 334 says: “You can’t deduct your own salary or any personal withdrawals you make from your business. As a sole proprietor, you are not an employee of the business.”
  • Single-member LLCs are treated the same by default. The IRS single-member LLC page says an LLC with one member that doesn’t elect corporate treatment is a “disregarded entity”, and an individual owner reports its activity on Schedule C, just like a sole proprietor.
  • Pay yourself by transfer, not by mixing accounts. Publication 583 advises making business payments by check and to “write checks payable to yourself only when making withdrawals from your business for personal use.” Today that usually means a bank transfer from your business account to your personal one. See our guide to a business bank account without an LLC.
  • Tax is on profit. Self-employment tax is 15.3% (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare) on 92.35% of your net earnings, per the IRS self-employment tax page and Publication 334. The Social Security part stops at the 2026 wage base of $184,500 (Topic 751). Income tax is on top of that.

Your draw doesn’t change your tax bill

Say your freelance business earns $84,000 in a year and spends $12,000 on business expenses. Your profit is $72,000. Self-employment tax is figured on 92.35% of that, $66,492.00, at 15.3%: about $10,173.28, and you can deduct half of it ($5,086.64) when you figure income tax. That number is the same whether you drew $72,000 during the year, $36,000 or nothing at all. The money you leave in the business account is still taxable profit.

The flip side: if you draw everything and spend it, the tax bill still arrives. That is the most common way freelancers get into trouble.

How much to pay yourself: a 5-step method

  1. Find your average monthly profit. Take the last 6 to 12 months of income minus business expenses and divide by the number of months. In our example, $72,000 ÷ 12 = $6,000 a month. New freelancers can use a conservative estimate until they have a few months of history.
  2. Take taxes off the top. Move a fixed percentage of every client payment into a separate tax savings account the day it arrives. We use 25% in this example ($1,500 a month); get your own percentage from our tax set-aside calculator. That money pays your quarterly estimated taxes.
  3. Pay yourself a fixed amount a bit below what’s left. After taxes, $6,000 leaves $4,500. Paying yourself about 80% of that, $3,600 a month, leaves $900 a month in the business. The 80% is our rule of thumb, not an IRS rule; the lumpier your income, the lower you should start.
  4. Build a buffer, then top up. Let the leftover build until the business holds a few months of draws and expenses. Three months in our example is 3 × ($3,600 + $1,000) = $13,800. Once the buffer is full, take the extra as a quarterly bonus draw or give yourself a raise. Our cash runway calculator shows how many months your buffer covers.
  5. Put it on a schedule. Pick fixed dates, such as the 1st and 15th, and set up an automatic transfer. Review the amount every quarter when you pay estimated taxes.

If you prefer percentages over a fixed paycheck, the Profit First method splits every payment into profit, owner’s pay, tax and operating accounts. Our Profit First calculator works out the splits.

Why a fixed draw beats “take what’s there”

Your personal account gets $3,600 every month even though income ranged from $3,000 to $9,000. The buffer absorbs the swings and never drops below $5,225. Over these six months the business brought in $36,000. After expenses and the tax set-aside that averages $3,750 a month, so a $3,600 draw still adds $900 to the buffer. Had the business started with no buffer, it would have been down to $225 by April, one slow month from missing a draw. That is why the buffer comes first.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Drawing the tax money. Your tax savings account isn’t pay. The IRS estimated taxes page says sole proprietors generally must make estimated payments if they expect to owe $1,000 or more. Most people avoid the underpayment penalty by paying at least 90% of this year’s tax or 100% of last year’s, whichever is smaller (110% of last year’s if your prior-year AGI was over $150,000, per the IRS estimated tax FAQ). See missed an estimated tax payment?
  • Recording your draw as an expense. In your books, a draw goes to an equity account (often called “owner’s draw”), not to expenses. If you put it in expenses, your profit looks smaller than it is and your tax estimate will be too low. Money you put into the business from personal savings is the reverse, an owner contribution, and isn’t income.
  • Paying personal bills straight from the business account. It isn’t illegal for a sole proprietor, but it turns tax time into an untangling job. Transfer the draw first, then pay personal bills from your personal account.
  • Taking an S corp election and keeping draws. Once you are an S corp, you need payroll and a reasonable salary. Distributions in place of salary are exactly what the IRS says it can reclassify.
  • Forgetting the slow months. If you set your draw from your best quarter, the buffer drains fast. Base it on a full year when you can.

FAQ

Can a sole proprietor pay themselves a salary?

Not in the payroll sense. IRS Publication 334 says that as a sole proprietor you are not an employee of the business and you can't deduct your own salary or personal withdrawals. You simply move money from the business to yourself; that transfer is called an owner's draw. A single-member LLC that hasn't elected to be taxed as a corporation works the same way for federal income tax.

Is an owner's draw taxed?

Not separately. You pay income tax and self-employment tax on the business's profit for the year, whether you draw all of it, some of it or none of it. That is why freelancers set aside a share of every payment for taxes before deciding how much to draw.

How much should I pay myself as a freelancer?

A common approach: take your average monthly profit over the last 6 to 12 months, subtract your tax set-aside, and pay yourself a fixed amount a little below what is left. The difference builds a buffer for slow months. In our example, $6,000 a month of profit minus a 25% tax set-aside leaves $4,500, and a $3,600 monthly draw leaves $900 a month for the buffer.

How often should I take an owner's draw?

There is no IRS rule. Many freelancers pick a schedule that matches their old paycheck, such as the 1st and 15th, because a regular, fixed amount makes personal budgeting easier than taking money whenever a client pays.

When does an owner's draw stop working?

If you elect S corporation tax treatment, the IRS expects you to pay yourself reasonable compensation as wages through payroll before taking other distributions, and it can reclassify distributions as wages if the salary is too low. Multi-member LLCs taxed as partnerships use distributions and guaranteed payments instead.
