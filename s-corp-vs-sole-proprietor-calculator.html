The short answer

As a sole proprietor (or single-member LLC taxed the default way) you pay 15.3% self-employment tax on 92.35% of your whole profit. With an S corporation election you pay yourself a reasonable salary, and only that salary carries Social Security and Medicare tax; the rest of the profit can come out as distributions without those taxes. The catch is the cost of running payroll, filing a separate business return and any state fees, plus a smaller qualified business income deduction. With $100,000 of profit, a $50,000 salary and $2,000 of extra costs, a single filer's 2026 federal bill is about $22,365 as a sole proprietor and $19,629 as an S corp (costs included), a difference of about $2,735 a year. The IRS guidance on S corporation reasonable compensation says an S corporation must pay a shareholder-employee reasonable compensation for services before non-wage distributions, and that the IRS can reclassify distributions as wages. The key question is where the money comes from: to the extent gross receipts come from your personal services, payments to you should be treated as wages. Factors the IRS lists include training and experience, duties, time and effort devoted to the business, what comparable businesses pay for similar services, and compensation agreements. For a one-person service business, that usually argues for a salary that looks like what you would pay someone else to do your job, not a token amount.

What actually changes with an S corp

  • Payroll taxes on salary only. You and the company each pay 6.2% Social Security (up to the $184,500 2026 wage base) and 1.45% Medicare (IRS Topic 751). The 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax applies to wages above $200,000, with no employer match. The company's half is a business expense.
  • Federal unemployment tax. FUTA is 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages, usually 0.6% after the state credit (IRS Topic 759), so about $42 a year. Your state unemployment tax is extra; put it in costs.
  • Distributions skip self-employment tax. Profit left after salary passes through on Schedule K-1 and is taxed at your individual income tax rates, but not Social Security or Medicare tax (IRS S corporations page). Compare the sole proprietor rule on the IRS self-employment tax page.
  • A smaller QBI deduction. The 20% qualified business income deduction applies to the K-1 profit, not your salary, so it shrinks. The calculator accounts for this.
  • More filings. Quarterly Form 941 and annual Form 940 for payroll, a W-2 for you, and Form 1120-S for the business. A calendar-year S corporation's return is due the 15th day of the 3rd month after year-end (Form 1120-S instructions), so March 15, 2027 for 2026.
  • Health insurance and retirement work differently. Premiums for a more-than-2% shareholder are deductible by the S corp and reported as W-2 wages, but generally not subject to Social Security or Medicare tax if paid under a plan for employees (IRS guidance on S corporation reasonable compensation). Retirement contributions such as a solo 401(k) are based on your W-2 salary, so a low salary also limits them (see solo 401(k) vs SEP IRA).
  • Future Social Security benefits. A lower salary means fewer earnings reported for Social Security, which can mean a smaller benefit later.

How the election works

An eligible corporation (domestic, no more than 100 shareholders, one class of stock, only allowable shareholders) elects S status on Form 2553, signed by all shareholders (IRS S corporations page). An LLC can also make the election. To take effect for a tax year, Form 2553 generally must be filed no more than 2 months and 15 days after the start of that year, or at any time during the year before (Form 2553 instructions); relief for late elections exists. Before filing, check your state: some states charge S corporations their own taxes or fees, and our LLC vs sole proprietor guide covers what forming an LLC does and does not change.

Counting the extra costs honestly

The $2,000 used above is an example, not a quote. Your real figure is payroll software or service, the extra cost of a business tax return, any state filing fees or entity taxes, and state unemployment tax. That $1,000 gap covers the tax return and bookkeeping side; payroll is separate.

How the calculator works

  1. Sole proprietor: self-employment tax on 92.35% of profit (12.4% up to $184,500, 2.9% Medicare, plus 0.9% above $200,000 single / $250,000 joint), then income tax on profit minus half of SE tax, the 2026 standard deduction and, if checked, the 20% QBI deduction.
  2. S corp: profit minus your extra costs, minus salary, the employer's 7.65% payroll tax and FUTA leaves the K-1 profit. Your side pays 7.65% on salary (plus 0.9% above the threshold). Income tax is on salary plus K-1 profit, minus the standard deduction and 20% of the K-1 profit as QBI.
  3. If the salary plus employer taxes would exceed the profit left after costs, the salary is lowered to the most the business could pay.
  4. Difference = sole proprietor federal tax minus (S corp payroll taxes + income tax + extra costs).

Not included: state income, franchise or unemployment taxes, credits, itemized deductions, other household income, health insurance and retirement contributions, the QBI limits above roughly $200,000 single / $400,000 joint of taxable income (the QBI deduction is left out above that), and the new $400 minimum QBI deduction for 2026. Sources checked October 29, 2026: IRS Topic 751 (6.2% / 1.45% rates, $184,500 wage base, Additional Medicare), IRS Topic 759 (FUTA 6.0% on $7,000, 0.6% after credit), the IRS guidance on S corporation reasonable compensation, the IRS S corporations page, Form 2553 instructions, Form 1120-S instructions, and Keeper pricing. FAQ

At what income does an S corp make sense?

There is no IRS cutoff. It depends on how much salary is reasonable for your work, what the extra payroll, tax-prep and state costs are, and your state's rules. In our 2026 federal-only example with a salary at half of profit and $2,000 of extra costs, the election breaks even near $30,000 of profit and saves more as profit rises, but a higher reasonable salary or higher costs push that point up quickly. Run your own numbers in the calculator and check them with a CPA.

What counts as a reasonable salary?

The IRS has no fixed percentage. It says an S corporation must pay a shareholder-employee reasonable compensation for their services before taking non-wage distributions, and lists factors such as training and experience, duties, time devoted to the business, and what comparable businesses pay for similar services. For a one-person service business, most of the profit comes from your own work, which pushes the reasonable salary up.

Does an S corp lower income tax?

Not much by itself. The main saving is on Social Security and Medicare tax, because profit paid out as distributions instead of salary is not subject to those taxes. The qualified business income deduction can actually shrink, because salary does not count as qualified business income.

Do I need an LLC to become an S corp?

No. An S corporation is a tax election, made on Form 2553, that an eligible corporation or an LLC that elects corporate treatment can file. Many freelancers form an LLC first and then make the election, but the paperwork and state rules vary, so check with your state and a tax professional.

What extra work does an S corp add?

You run payroll for yourself (with quarterly Form 941 and annual Form 940 filings), file a separate business return on Form 1120-S, and give yourself a W-2 and a Schedule K-1. Many states also have their own S corporation filings, fees or taxes.
