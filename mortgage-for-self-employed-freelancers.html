Getting a Mortgage as a Self-Employed Freelancer

By Freelance Ledger · Published October 17, 2026 · How we research

Getting a mortgage when you're self-employed isn't impossible, but the paperwork is different. Instead of pay stubs, a lender builds your income from your tax returns and business records. Here is what the official sources say lenders typically do. Every lender has its own rules, so use this as a checklist of questions, not a promise of how any one lender will treat you.

Lenders must verify the income they rely on

Under the CFPB's ability-to-repay rule, a lender must make a reasonable, good-faith determination that you can repay a mortgage, and it must verify the income it relies on with reasonably reliable third-party records. The CFPB's compliance guide lists IRS tax-return transcripts, copies of filed tax returns, and bank account statements among the acceptable records. If a lender relies on deposits in your account, it must be able to confirm they are your personal income and not something like loan proceeds. (CFPB ATR/QM guide)

Two years of tax returns

For loans sold to Fannie Mae, the Selling Guide says lenders generally need a two-year history of earnings, and may verify self-employed income with signed federal tax returns filed with the IRS for the past two years, with all schedules attached (or IRS-issued transcripts instead). Fannie Mae treats anyone with a 25% or greater ownership interest in a business as self-employed. Business returns may also be needed in some cases, for example for a partnership or S corporation. As a sole proprietor or single-member LLC reporting on Schedule C, your business income is reported on your personal return. (Fannie Mae Selling Guide, B3-3.2-01; IRS, About Schedule C)

There are exceptions. Fannie Mae allows less than two years of self-employment history in limited cases, if the most recent returns show a full year of self-employment income and the file documents prior income at the same or greater level in a similar field. It also permits one year of returns only when the business has existed for five years and the borrower has owned at least 25% for five years. Don't count on these; they depend on the lender and the loan. Freddie Mac's consumer site similarly says most lenders want at least two years of consistent self-employment, and that a W-2 from a previous employer may help if you haven't been self-employed two full years. (Freddie Mac, qualifying when self-employed)

Why net income, after deductions, is what counts

Schedule C reports your profit or loss from the business. The income a lender can use comes from what the return shows, so expenses you deduct reduce the income on paper. That is the tradeoff: write-offs lower your tax bill, but they can also lower the income a lender will count. Fannie Mae's guide notes that the goal of a self-employment cash-flow analysis is to find the income that can be relied on to qualify. If you plan to buy within a couple of years, talk to a tax professional before choosing between maximum deductions and higher reported income. Our self-employment tax calculator shows how profit drives your tax bill.

Cash flow analysis (Fannie Mae Form 1084)

Fannie Mae says lenders must prepare a written analysis of a self-employed borrower's personal income, and may use its Cash Flow Analysis (Form 1084), another cash flow analysis, or an automated tool such as Fannie Mae's Income Calculator, that applies the same principles. The Selling Guide also describes a separate business income analysis (the Comparative Income Analysis, Form 1088) that looks at year-to-year trends in a business's gross income, expenses and taxable income, unless the lender may waive business returns. Lenders look at the stability of your income, the nature of the business, demand for your service, and the business's financial strength. In practice that means income that is steady or growing is easier to qualify on than income that dropped recently. A lender may adjust your reported figures, so the number it uses may differ from the profit on your return. (Fannie Mae Selling Guide, B3-3.2-01)

Bank-statement loans and other alternatives

Some lenders offer programs that use bank statements instead of tax returns. These aren't a single standard product: eligibility, how many months of statements, how deposits are analyzed, rates and fees differ by lender, and they may cost more than a loan underwritten from tax returns, so compare the full terms. The CFPB's compliance guide confirms bank statements are an acceptable way to verify income, and that the lender must still confirm deposits are really your income and still decide in good faith that you can repay. Ask any lender offering one what documents it needs, what its rate, fees and any prepayment terms are, and how it counts income. Read your Loan Estimate carefully, and compare offers from several lenders. The CFPB advises contacting multiple lenders and gathering your documents early. (CFPB, exploring your loan choices)

What to gather

  • Federal tax returns for the last two years, with all schedules (Schedule C or K-1s as they apply)
  • Any business returns, if your business files separately (Forms 1065, 1120 or 1120S)
  • A year-to-date profit and loss statement, if the lender asks for one
  • Recent personal and business bank statements
  • Business license, 1099 forms, and proof of the length of your self-employment, where relevant

Fannie Mae's consumer site lists two years of tax returns, 1099s for contract workers, profit and loss statements and a business license among documents lenders may request, and Freddie Mac's lists letters from clients, a CPA statement and business insurance as ways to show consistent self-employment. Lenders vary, so ask for their exact list. (Fannie Mae, documents you need)

Practical steps before you apply

  1. Look at your own numbers first. Add up net profit from the last two years and see what monthly income that implies.
  2. Estimate the payment. Use the mortgage calculator to see the full monthly cost, including taxes, insurance and any HOA dues.
  3. Keep business and personal accounts separate. It makes bank statements easier to document. See our business bank account guide.
  4. Keep returns filed and records organized. Lenders work from filed returns, so plan around your estimated tax schedule and your 1099 forms.
  5. Talk to more than one lender and ask each how it treats self-employed income.

FAQ

Do I really need two years of tax returns?

Most lenders ask for two years, and Fannie Mae generally requires a two-year history. There are limited exceptions, and the rules differ by lender and loan type.

Will lenders use my gross income?

Generally no. Self-employed income is analyzed from your tax returns and business records, after expenses. A lender calculates its own qualifying figure.

Can a bank-statement loan replace tax returns?

Some lenders offer them, and the CFPB's guide lists bank statements as acceptable records for verifying income. Terms and costs vary, so compare them closely against other options.

Will I be approved?

We can't say. Approval depends on your credit, debts, savings, down payment, the property and the lender's rules, not just your income documentation.
