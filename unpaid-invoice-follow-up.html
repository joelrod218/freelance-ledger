Client Hasn't Paid? A Freelancer's Unpaid-Invoice Follow-Up Schedule, From Day 1 to Small Claims

An invoice is a few days overdue and your client has gone quiet. The words are the easy part; we have five late payment email templates for that. The harder question is what to do when: how often to follow up, when to call, when to stop working, and what you can actually do if the money still doesn't come. This page is that plan, from the day before the due date to small claims court, with the laws that can help in some states and the tax rule most freelancers get wrong.

The short version

  • Follow up early and on a schedule. Day 1, day 7, a phone call by day 14. Most late invoices are lost emails or stuck approvals.
  • Automate the polite part. FreshBooks, Bonsai, Wave and Zoho Invoice can all send reminders for you.
  • Pause work before the balance gets scary, in writing, with a date, ideally backed by your contract.
  • Send a formal demand before you sue. California's courts say you must ask for payment first, and it's good practice everywhere.
  • Know your state. New York State, New York City and Illinois have freelancer laws that set a 30-day default payment deadline and give you a free complaint process. In New York, a client that doesn't pay on time can owe double damages.
  • Small claims limits vary a lot: $8,000 in Florida, $12,500 for a sole proprietor in California ($6,250 for an LLC), $20,000 in Texas.
  • Unpaid isn't taxable, and isn't deductible. On the cash method you don't report money you never received, so there's no bad-debt write-off either.

Before you chase: rule out the boring reasons

A surprising share of "late" invoices are stuck on the client's side for fixable reasons. Before your first follow-up, check:

  • Did it reach the payer? Your contact may have approved it and never forwarded it. Ask who in accounts payable should be copied.
  • Does it have what their system needs? PO number, vendor number, project code, their legal entity name.
  • Do they have your W-9? Many companies won't release payment to a new vendor without one. See our W-9 guide.
  • Are the payment details right, and is there an easy way to pay (a card or ACH link, not just "send a check")?
  • Is the due date clear? "Net 30" from what date? Our free invoice generator prints an exact due date and an optional late-fee line.

What changes the schedule

  • The client disputes the work. Stop sending payment reminders and deal with the dispute directly. Ask what specifically is wrong, offer a fix if it's fair, and get the outcome in writing. Reminders on a disputed invoice just harden positions.
  • They ask for more time. Agree to a specific date or a short payment plan (for example two payments two weeks apart), and confirm it by email.
  • They offer to pay less to settle. That's a negotiation, and you can say no. New York State's freelancer law (below) goes further: once you've started the work, a client can't make accepting less than the contracted amount a condition of paying you on time.
  • They stop responding entirely. Skip ahead to the final notice. Silence after two follow-ups and a call is an answer.

Pausing work and late fees

Pausing work is often the strongest lever you have, especially on ongoing retainers. Do it in writing, give a date, and say what restarts the work. It's cleanest when your contract already says that work may pause if an invoice is more than a set number of days overdue. Don't hold back finished work you've already been paid for, and don't delete or "break" anything you've delivered. That turns a payment problem into a dispute about you.

Late fees work when they were agreed up front, in the contract or the accepted invoice terms. A fee that appears for the first time on the third reminder is hard to enforce and usually slows payment down. Keep it modest (a flat fee or a low monthly percentage) because state laws can cap interest. If a client pays the original amount quickly once you ask, waiving the fee can be a cheap way to keep the relationship.

The final notice (demand letter) checklist

A demand letter is a formal, final request for payment before you escalate. California's courts require you to ask the other side for the money before filing in small claims, if possible. Its SC-100-INFO sheet says a plaintiff "must first make a demand," and its self-help guide says you can ask in person, by letter or by email. Elsewhere it's still smart: it often gets you paid, and it shows a judge you tried. Include:

  1. Your name and business name, and the client's legal name and mailing address.
  2. The amount owed, each invoice number, invoice date and due date.
  3. One sentence on what was agreed and delivered (reference the contract or the email where they approved the work).
  4. The dates you already followed up.
  5. Any agreed late fee, calculated, kept separate from the principal.
  6. A firm deadline, such as 10 business days, and how to pay (link, ACH details).
  7. Exactly what you'll do if it isn't paid: file a complaint under your state's freelancer law, file in small claims court, or refer it for collection. Only name steps you'll actually take.

Keep the tone factual, not angry, and send it by email plus a trackable method. Template 5 on our late payment templates page is a shorter final notice you can build from.

Freelancer payment laws: NY, NYC and Illinois

Escalation options after the deadline

Small claims court

Small claims is built for people without lawyers, and an unpaid invoice with a paper trail is a typical case. Limits as of November 3, 2026:

StateSmall claims limitNotes
Florida$8,000Not counting costs, interest and attorneys' fees. Starts with a Statement of Claim ("work done/materials furnished" is one of the standard claim types), filed through the state e-filing portal or with the county clerk (DIY Florida; Fla. Sm. Cl. R. 7.010)
California$12,500 for a natural person, including a sole proprietor; $6,250 for a business entityFiling fee $30 to $100; no lawyer in court; must ask for payment first; no more than two claims over $2,500 each per calendar year (CA self-help, SC-100-INFO)
Texas$20,000Justice court; the limit includes attorney fees but not statutory interest and court costs (Texas State Law Library)

Two things to know before you file. First, winning isn't collecting. California's self-help guide says that if you win, you have to collect the money yourself; the court doesn't do it for you. A client with no assets, or one that ignores the judgment, can cost you more time. Second, the entity matters. If you freelance through an LLC in California, the lower $6,250 business limit applies. For other states, search your state court's self-help site for "small claims."

Collection agency or lawyer's letter

Collection agencies commonly work on contingency, keeping a share of what they recover, and a lawyer's demand letter costs a flat fee or an hourly rate. Both can make sense for larger invoices. Get the cost in writing first and compare it with the amount owed. For a few hundred dollars, a well-documented small claims filing, or a write-off, is often cheaper.

Writing it off

Sometimes the honest answer is that chasing costs more than the invoice. If you stop, stop cleanly: send a last note closing the account, keep your records, and don't do more work for that client without payment up front.

Taxes: an unpaid invoice isn't income, or a deduction

Most freelancers use the cash method. Under it, IRS Publication 334 says you include in income "all items of income you actually or constructively receive during your tax year." An invoice that's still unpaid on December 31 isn't income for that year. You report it when the money arrives. One year-end catch: a valid check you receive by December 31 counts as income for that year even if you can't deposit it until January.

The flip side is that you can't write off a client who never pays. IRS Topic 453 says that if you're a cash method taxpayer, "you generally can't take a bad debt deduction for unpaid salaries, wages, rents, fees" and similar items. Pub 334 says the same for business bad debts: no deduction for amounts you never included in income. You already got the tax benefit by never reporting the income. Only if you use the accrual method and already reported the invoice can a business bad debt deduction apply. Your costs of chasing the debt (postage, a small claims filing fee) are a separate question, so ask a tax pro.

Unpaid invoices also distort your quarterly estimates. If you set aside tax from invoices you issued rather than payments you received, you may be over-saving. Our tax set-aside calculator works from money actually received.

FAQ

How long should I wait before following up on an unpaid invoice?

Don't wait at all. Send a short check-in the day after the due date. Most late payments are a missed email or a stuck approval, not a refusal, and a fast, friendly nudge fixes those. Then follow up about once a week, and pick up the phone by around two weeks overdue.

When should I stop working for a client who hasn't paid?

When your contract says you can, and before the unpaid balance grows past what you can afford to lose. A common point is 14 to 30 days overdue. Tell the client in writing, give a date, and say exactly what will restart the work (payment of the overdue invoice). Pausing is easier to defend if your contract has a pause or suspension clause.

Can I charge a late fee on an overdue invoice?

Only if the client agreed to it, usually in your contract or on the accepted terms of the invoice. Adding a surprise fee after the fact is hard to enforce and tends to slow payment down. FreshBooks and Bonsai can add late fees automatically, and state laws can limit how much interest you can charge, so keep the rate modest and check your state.

What is the small claims limit for unpaid freelance invoices?

It depends on your state. As of November 3, 2026, Florida small claims covers claims up to $8,000 (not counting costs, interest and attorneys' fees). California allows $12,500 for a natural person, including a sole proprietor, but $6,250 for a business entity such as an LLC. Texas justice courts hear small claims up to $20,000, including any attorney fees. Winning doesn't mean the court collects for you; California's courts, for example, say collecting is up to you.

Can I deduct an unpaid invoice as a bad debt?

Usually not. Most freelancers use the cash method, and IRS Topic 453 says cash-method taxpayers generally can't take a bad debt deduction for unpaid fees. Because you never reported the unpaid amount as income, there's nothing to write off, and you also don't owe tax on money you never received. If you use the accrual method and already reported the invoice as income, a business bad debt deduction may be available.
