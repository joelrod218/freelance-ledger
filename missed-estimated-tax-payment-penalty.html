Missed an Estimated Tax Payment? The Penalty, the $1,000 Rule and How to Fix It

By Freelance Ledger · Published October 30, 2026 · How we research

Rules checked on October 30, 2026 against the IRS estimated taxes page, its underpayment penalty page, the 2025 Instructions for Form 2210, Publication 505 (2026), the 2026 Form 1040-ES and the IRS fourth-quarter 2026 interest rate announcement (IR-2026-98). Federal rules only; states have their own. General information, not tax advice.

Two questions come up every quarter: "Do I even have to pay estimated tax if I owe under $1,000?" and "I missed a payment. How bad is it?" Short answers: the $1,000 rule is narrower than it sounds, and a missed payment usually costs far less than people fear, as long as you pay it soon. This page covers the edge cases. For the basics (the four due dates, how to estimate what you owe, how to pay), start with our quarterly estimated taxes guide.

The short version

  • Under $1,000 after withholding: no estimated payments needed and no penalty. Estimated payments you made don't count toward that $1,000 test.
  • Missed a payment: the penalty is the unpaid amount × 7% a year (the IRS underpayment rate for Oct–Dec 2026) × the days it was late. Missing $2,000.00 for 45 days costs about $17.26.
  • Fix it now: pay the missed amount today through IRS payments (Direct Pay or your online account). That stops the clock on that shortfall.
  • Have a W-2 job too? Raising withholding before year-end can cover earlier shortfalls, because withholding counts as paid evenly through the year.
  • No need to file Form 2210 in most cases. The IRS figures the penalty and mails a bill.

Do you owe estimated tax if you'll owe under $1,000?

The IRS estimated taxes page says individuals, including sole proprietors, "generally have to make estimated tax payments if they expect to owe tax of $1,000 or more when their return is filed." The Form 2210 instructions turn that into a penalty exception: you won't owe the penalty if the total tax on your return minus the tax paid through withholding is less than $1,000 (refundable credits are subtracted too).

Three things people miss:

  • It's about tax, not income. $1,000 of tax can come from a modest amount of freelance profit, because self-employment tax (15.3% on 92.35% of profit) starts once net earnings reach $400. Our self-employment tax calculator shows how fast it adds up.
  • Estimated payments don't count toward the $1,000 test. Only withholding and refundable credits come off. A full-time freelancer with $3,000 of tax and no withholding doesn't qualify for this exception, even after paying $2,500 in estimates. They're judged on the safe harbors below instead.
  • It works best with a day job. If your W-2 withholding covers nearly all of your tax and the freelance work leaves less than $1,000 extra, you can skip quarterly payments and pay the rest in April.

Other ways out of the penalty

  • No tax last year. You don't owe the penalty (or have to pay estimated tax) if you had no tax liability for the prior year, were a U.S. citizen or resident alien all year, and that prior year covered 12 months. "No tax liability" means your total tax was zero or you didn't have to file. This often covers a first year of freelancing right after school.
  • Safe harbor. No penalty if your withholding plus on-time estimated payments covered at least the smaller of 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's (110% if last year's AGI was over $150,000, or $75,000 married filing separately). For 2026 the 110% test uses your 2025 AGI.
  • Farmers and fishers. If at least two-thirds of your gross income for 2025 or 2026 is from farming or fishing, Publication 505 gives you one 2026 due date, January 15, 2027, and the 90% figure drops to 66⅔%. Skip estimates entirely by filing and paying in full by March 1, 2027. These folks use Form 2210-F.

What a missed payment actually costs

The IRS underpayment penalty page says it figures the penalty from the amount you underpaid, how long it was underpaid, and the published quarterly interest rate for underpayments. The Form 2210 penalty worksheet makes it plain arithmetic: underpayment × days late ÷ 365 × the rate for each period. The rate was 6% for April–June 2026 and is 7% for July–September and October–December 2026 (IRS quarterly interest rates; IR-2026-98, Aug. 21, 2026).

So the penalty is real but not catastrophic. Skipping a whole year of $8,000 in payments costs a few hundred dollars. The bigger risk is the April bill: if you haven't set the money aside, you can end up with a balance you can't pay, and that brings a separate penalty (below).

Missed one? Do this now

  1. Pay the missed amount as soon as you can. The penalty runs day by day until the shortfall is paid. Pay online via IRS Direct Pay or your IRS online account (the estimated taxes page lists every method), and choose estimated tax for 2026.
  2. Know where it goes. The Form 2210 instructions say payments go first to the earliest underpaid installment, even if you mark them for a later one. Catching up now fixes the old shortfall first. Then you still need the next payment by its due date.
  3. Have a W-2 job, or a spouse with one if you file jointly? Ask the employer for extra withholding on a new Form W-4 for the rest of the year. Withholding counts as paid in equal amounts on each due date unless you choose otherwise, so a big December withholding can reach back and cover a missed April or June payment. Estimated payments can't do that.
  4. Lumpy income? If most of your money came late in the year, the annualized income installment method (Form 2210, Schedule AI) can shrink or erase the penalty for the earlier quarters. You have to file Form 2210 to use it.
  5. Use the January shortcut if it fits. The 2026 Form 1040-ES says you can skip the January 15, 2027 payment if you file your 2026 return by February 1, 2027 and pay the whole balance with it. (Publication 505 says January 31; that's a Sunday, so the next business day applies.) This only covers the fourth installment. Any penalty for the first three still applies.
  6. Fix the system for 2027. Move a percentage of every client payment into a tax account (our tax set-aside calculator gives you the number), and put the 2027 due dates in your calendar.

How the IRS handles it

  • You usually don't have to do anything. Per the Form 2210 instructions, the IRS will generally figure the penalty and send a bill. Leave the penalty line on your return blank. File your return by April 15 and pay the bill by its due date, and no interest is added to the penalty.
  • When you must file Form 2210: to request a full or partial waiver (Part II, box A or B), to use the annualized income method (box C), or to treat withholding as paid when it was actually withheld (box D). For a 2026 underpayment you'll use the 2026 version of Form 2210 when it's posted. The 2025 instructions are the latest available today.
  • A refund doesn't cancel it. The IRS says you can owe the penalty for a period you underpaid "even if you are due a refund" when you file. Each due date is judged separately.

Can the penalty be waived?

Per the Form 2210 instructions and the IRS penalty page, all or part of the penalty can be waived if:

  • the underpayment came from a casualty, disaster or other unusual circumstance and charging it would be inequitable (in a federally declared disaster area, the IRS may postpone deadlines and applies the relief automatically, so you usually don't file Form 2210 for it), or
  • you retired after reaching age 62 or became disabled during the tax year or the year before, and the underpayment was due to reasonable cause, not willful neglect.

The IRS says this penalty generally can't be waived for reasonable cause alone. "I forgot" or "cash was tight" won't qualify. To ask for a waiver, check box A or B on Form 2210 and attach a statement explaining why and for which period, plus documents (for example insurance or police reports, or proof of your retirement date and age). If the IRS gave you wrong written advice in response to your written request, there's a separate route on the penalty page.

Don't mix it up with the failure-to-pay penalty

The estimated tax penalty covers paying too little during the year. If you then can't pay the balance by the April filing deadline, the failure-to-pay penalty applies too: 0.5% of the unpaid tax for each month or part of a month, capped at 25%, plus interest. The IRS cuts it to 0.25% a month during an approved payment plan if you filed on time. If April will be tight, file on time anyway, pay what you can, and set up a payment plan.

FAQ

Do I have to pay estimated tax if I owe less than $1,000?

Generally no. The IRS says individuals, including sole proprietors, generally must make estimated payments if they expect to owe $1,000 or more when they file, and the Form 2210 instructions say there's no underpayment penalty if the tax on your return minus your withholding is less than $1,000. Note the test subtracts withholding (and refundable credits), not your estimated payments, so it mostly helps people with a W-2 job and a small side business.

What happens if I miss a quarterly estimated tax payment?

The IRS can charge an underpayment penalty that works like interest: the unpaid amount times the IRS underpayment rate (7% a year for October to December 2026) for the number of days it was late. A $2,000 payment due September 15, 2026 and paid October 30, 2026 costs about $17.26. Pay as soon as you can, because the penalty stops growing once the shortfall is paid.

Can I avoid the penalty if I get a refund when I file?

Not necessarily. The IRS says you may be charged a penalty for a payment period you underpaid even if you're due a refund when you file. The penalty is figured separately for each due date, so paying enough later in the year doesn't erase an earlier shortfall. Withholding is the exception: it's treated as paid in equal amounts on each due date unless you show otherwise.

Do I need to file Form 2210?

Usually not. The Form 2210 instructions say the IRS will generally figure the penalty and send you a bill. You must file it yourself if you're asking for a waiver, using the annualized income method, or treating withholding as paid on the dates it was actually withheld. If you file your return by April 15 and pay the bill by its due date, the IRS doesn't charge interest on the penalty.

Can the estimated tax penalty be waived?

Sometimes. The IRS may waive it if the underpayment was due to a casualty, disaster or other unusual circumstance and the penalty would be inequitable, or if you retired after age 62 or became disabled in the tax year or the year before and had reasonable cause. The IRS says the penalty generally can't be waived just for reasonable cause otherwise. You request a waiver on Form 2210 with an explanation and documents.
