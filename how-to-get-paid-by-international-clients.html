Short answer: for a US freelancer, the cheapest way to get paid by a client in another country is almost always a bank transfer: either a wire straight to your US bank, or a local transfer in the client’s currency into account details from a service like Wise or Payoneer, which then converts it and sends US dollars to your bank. Cards and PayPal are the most convenient for the client but the most expensive for you: on a $2,000 invoice they cost about $80 to $150 once the international and currency-conversion fees are added, against roughly $0 to $31 for the bank-transfer routes. Whatever you choose, put the currency and payment method in your contract, and report the income in US dollars.

Your options, in plain English

1. A wire to your US business bank

The simplest setup: ask your bank for its incoming international wire instructions and put them on your invoice. Check your bank’s fee schedule first. Novo’s fee schedule, for example, lists incoming foreign wires at $0.00. Ask the client to send US dollars, so the conversion happens on their side, and, if you want the full amount to arrive, to choose the option where the sender pays all fees (often labeled “OUR”). Our business bank account without an LLC guide covers opening an account as a sole proprietor.

2. Wise: local account details in several currencies

Wise gives you account details so clients can pay you “like a local”: its receiving page lists free domestic receiving in AUD, CAD, EUR, GBP, HUF, NZD, PHP, SGD and USD, with fixed fees for Swift payments (6.11 USD for USD wires, 2.39 EUR for EUR Swift, 2.16 GBP for GBP Swift). On a Wise Business account, the business pricing page lists 31 USD for getting account details, and conversion fees “from 0.23%” depending on the currency. On Nov 19, 2026, Wise’s calculator quoted 0.47% to convert euros to dollars, 0.36% for pounds and 0.48% for Canadian dollars, plus a small fixed fee when sending to your US bank (€1.03 on the euro quote). Wise’s receiving page explains that it doesn’t lend out customer money and instead safeguards it, which is different from how a bank holds deposits.

3. Payoneer: receiving accounts and marketplace payouts

Payoneer’s pricing page lists receiving into a local-currency account as free, receiving into an account in a currency that isn’t your local one at 1% (minimum $1.00), card payments at up to 3.99% + $0.49, ACH debits from US payers at 1%, moving money between your currency balances at 0.50%, and withdrawals to a US bank in US dollars at $1.50. It also lists a $29.95 annual account fee if you receive less than $6,000 in any 12 consecutive months. Payoneer says the exact fee for your account is shown before you confirm each transaction. Payoneer also receives payouts from marketplaces and integrated platforms (the fee varies by marketplace).

4. Cards through Stripe

Stripe’s pricing is 2.9% + 30¢ for domestic cards, plus 1.5% for international cards and 1% more if currency conversion is required. If you send invoices through Stripe Invoicing, its pricing page adds 0.4% per paid invoice on Starter (0.5% on Plus). If your invoicing app processes cards through Stripe, check whether it passes on Stripe’s international surcharge; our free invoicing software comparison lists each app’s domestic fees.

5. PayPal

PayPal’s US fee page (updated Nov 4, 2026) charges invoices paid by card at 2.99% + a fixed fee and invoices paid with PayPal at 3.49% + a fixed fee, plus 1.50% for international transactions. The fixed fee depends on the currency you receive ($0.49 for US dollars, €0.39 for euros, £0.39 for pounds). If PayPal converts the money, the exchange rate includes a 3.00% spread on most transactions. Withdrawing to your linked bank account is free when no conversion is involved; instant transfers cost 1.50%.

Put the payment terms in writing

  • Currency: state the invoice currency (“all amounts in US dollars”).
  • Method: list the accepted methods and the details (wire instructions, or local account details for their country).
  • Fees: say who pays transfer and conversion fees. Ask for wires with all fees paid by the sender if you want the full amount.
  • Due date and late payment: the same terms you use with US clients. Our unpaid invoice follow-up plan and late payment email templates work for overseas clients too, though US small claims courts and state freelancer laws may be hard to use against a client abroad.

The tax side (US freelancers)

  • It’s all taxable. The IRS says US citizens and resident aliens are “subject to tax on worldwide income from all sources” (IRS). Publication 334 says you must report all business income, “including income not reported to you on a Form 1099.” A client abroad may never send you a 1099, so track these payments yourself.
  • Report it in dollars. “You must express the amounts you report on your U.S. tax return in U.S. dollars,” using “the exchange rate prevailing when you receive, pay, or accrue the item” (IRS foreign currency page). The IRS “has no official exchange rate” and “generally … accepts any posted exchange rate that is used consistently”; it also publishes yearly average rates. If Wise, Payoneer or PayPal converts the payment the day you receive it, the dollar amount you got is a practical record, but keep the original-currency amount too.
  • Fees are a business expense. Record the full invoice amount as income and the transfer and conversion fees as an expense. Publication 334 allows business expenses that are “ordinary and necessary” for your business.
  • Form W-8BEN isn’t for you. The IRS says to give Form W-8BEN to the payer “if you are a foreign person” (About Form W-8BEN). The Form W-9 instructions say “Use Form W-9 only if you are a U.S. person (including a resident alien).” If a client sends you a W-8BEN, tell them you are a US person. See our W-9 guide.
  • Foreign accounts over $10,000. A US person with a financial interest in or signature authority over financial accounts outside the United States must file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) if their combined value exceeded $10,000 at any time during the year; it is due April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15 (IRS FBAR page). Whether a particular multi-currency account counts as a foreign account depends on where and by whom it is held, so ask a tax professional if you keep large balances abroad.
  • Estimated taxes still apply. Foreign-client income goes into the same Schedule C profit and quarterly estimates as everything else; see quarterly estimated taxes and the tax set-aside calculator.

FAQ

What is the cheapest way to get paid by a client abroad?

Usually a bank transfer, not a card. In our $2,000 example (fees checked Nov 19, 2026), a client paying into Wise US-dollar account details by local transfer cost about $1.13 to move the money to your US bank, and a USD wire into Wise cost $7.24 in total. Card payments through Payoneer, Stripe or PayPal cost about $80 to $150 on the same invoice once international and currency-conversion fees are added.

Should I invoice in US dollars or the client's currency?

If you invoice in US dollars, the client handles the currency conversion and you know exactly what you will receive. If you invoice in their currency, paying is easier for them, but you take the exchange-rate risk and pay a conversion fee. A simple default is to invoice in USD unless the client asks otherwise, and then use a service with local account details in the client's currency.

Do I pay US tax on income from foreign clients?

Yes, if you are a US citizen or resident. The IRS says US citizens and resident aliens are taxed on worldwide income, and Publication 334 says you must report all business income, including income not reported to you on a Form 1099. Report it in US dollars using the exchange rate when you received the payment; the IRS has no official exchange rate and generally accepts any posted rate used consistently.

A foreign client asked me to fill out Form W-8BEN. Should I?

Form W-8BEN is for foreign persons. If you are a US citizen or resident, the Form W-9 instructions say to use Form W-9 only if you are a US person, so tell the client you are a US person and offer a W-9 instead. If you are unsure of your status, ask a tax professional.

Does PayPal charge more for international payments?

Yes. PayPal's US merchant fee page (updated Nov 4, 2026) adds 1.50% to the domestic rate for international commercial and invoicing transactions, plus a fixed fee based on the currency received ($0.49 for US dollars). If PayPal converts the currency for you, its exchange rate includes a 3.00% conversion spread on most transactions.
