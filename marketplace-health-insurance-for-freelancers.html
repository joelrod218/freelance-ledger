Marketplace Health Insurance for Freelancers: Estimating Income, the 400% Cliff and Form 1095-A

By Freelance Ledger · Published November 27, 2026 · How we research

Rules checked on November 27, 2026 against the IRS premium tax credit Q&A, the IRS notice that repayment caps were eliminated, the draft 2026 Form 8962 instructions (Oct. 20, 2026), Rev. Proc. 2026-26, the 2026 HHS poverty guidelines and HealthCare.gov's pages on self-employment income, dates and Form 1095-A. Federal rules; some states run their own Marketplace and add their own subsidies. General information, not tax or insurance advice.

If you don't have a spouse's job plan to join, the ACA Marketplace (HealthCare.gov or a state exchange) is where many freelancers buy coverage. The premium tax credit can cut the cost a lot, but it's based on a guess: your income for a year that hasn't happened yet. For employees that guess is easy. For freelancers it's the whole game, and the rules got stricter in 2026. This page covers how to estimate, what changed, what happens at tax time, and the levers you have.

The short version

  • Open enrollment for 2027 is on now. HealthCare.gov: enroll by December 15 for coverage starting January 1; open enrollment ends January 15 (coverage from February 1).
  • The 400% cliff is back. From tax year 2026, household income above 400% of the federal poverty line means no premium tax credit. For 2027 coverage that's $63,840 for a single person, $132,000 for a family of four.
  • No more repayment cap. From tax year 2026, if advance payments exceed the credit you qualify for, you repay all of the difference.
  • You estimate net profit, not gross receipts: Schedule C income minus expenses, plus other household income.
  • Update the Marketplace during the year when your income forecast changes. That's the main way to avoid a surprise bill.
  • Form 1095-A arrives by mid-February; you need it to file Form 8962.

What changed in 2026 (and carries into 2027)

From 2021 through 2025, temporary "enhanced" credits let people above 400% of the poverty line qualify and lowered what everyone else paid. The IRS premium tax credit Q&A (Q7) describes that expansion as covering tax years 2021 through 2025; it was not extended. The draft 2026 Form 8962 instructions list what's new:

  • Income cap: "For tax years beginning after 2025, taxpayers whose household income exceeds 400% of the federal poverty line for their family size are not eligible for the premium tax credit."
  • Repayment limit eliminated: if advance payments exceed the credit, "the taxpayer must repay the full excess amount." The old caps based on income no longer apply. The IRS also posted a separate notice, Repayment caps eliminated, attributing the change to the Working Families Tax Cuts Act.
  • Income-based special enrollment: no credit for coverage picked up through a special enrollment period based only on income, unless it's tied to a qualifying life event or another change HHS specifies.
  • Below 100%: household income under the poverty line generally means no credit, with an exception if the Marketplace estimated you'd be at 100%–400% when you enrolled and advance payments were made.

Credits are also smaller inside the range: in 2026 the expected contribution toward the benchmark plan runs from 2.10% to 9.96% of household income (draft 2026 instructions, Table 2), and for 2027 from 2.15% to 10.22% (Rev. Proc. 2026-26). If you're above 400%, you can still buy a Marketplace plan at full price.

How to estimate freelance income for the application

HealthCare.gov's self-employment page says to report your net self-employment income ("Net income is sometimes called 'profit'"), the figure that ends up on Schedule C, and that savings are based on "your estimated net income for the year you're getting coverage, not last year's income." In practice:

  1. Start from last year's Schedule C profit and adjust for clients you've gained or lost, rate changes and planned time off. HealthCare.gov suggests using "past experience, realistic expectations, industry standards."
  2. Subtract your business expenses the same way you would on Schedule C (HealthCare.gov: the deductible items allowed on Schedule C).
  3. Add other household income: a spouse's wages, interest and dividends (including tax-exempt interest), capital gains, most retirement withdrawals, unemployment. The Marketplace's measure is MAGI: AGI plus untaxed foreign income, nontaxable Social Security and tax-exempt interest (what to include).
  4. Leave a margin if you're near 400%. Under the new rules, landing just above the line costs you the entire credit. If your best guess is $60,000 and the line is $63,840, one good month can push you over.
  5. Keep records that back it up. If the Marketplace asks you to confirm income, it accepts a "self-employment ledger": a spreadsheet, accounting-software report or handwritten book of income and expenses. Our expense tracker template works.

Levers that lower household income

For the credit, household income is your modified AGI (IRS Q8). Anything that lowers AGI lowers it:

  • Business expenses. Every legitimate Schedule C deduction lowers profit. See our freelance tax deductions checklist.
  • Retirement contributions. Deductible SEP-IRA and traditional solo 401(k) contributions reduce AGI. SEP contributions for a year can be made up to the return's due date, including extensions (IRS SEP FAQs), so you can decide after year-end with real numbers. Roth contributions don't help here. Limits: solo 401(k) vs SEP IRA.
  • Half of self-employment tax is deducted automatically in AGI.
  • The self-employed health insurance deduction. You can deduct the premiums you actually paid (after the credit). Because the deduction and the credit affect each other, Publication 974 has worksheets to solve both; see our self-employed health insurance deduction guide.
  • HSA contributions if you're in an HSA-eligible plan.

These are legitimate tax moves, not tricks, but the line is hard. Don't push income into next year in ways that don't match how your business actually works, and remember the Marketplace application asks for an honest estimate. The draft instructions also warn that the below-100% exception doesn't protect you if you gave the Marketplace incorrect information "with intentional or reckless disregard for the facts."

During the year: report changes

HealthCare.gov's reporting changes page says to update your application "as soon as possible" when income or household changes. If your income estimate goes up and you don't report it, "you'll have to pay the difference back." If it goes down, you may qualify for more savings, or for Medicaid/CHIP. The IRS lists the changes to report: household income, address, employment, gaining or losing other coverage, birth or adoption, marriage or divorce. A habit that works: check year-to-date profit against your estimate every quarter, at the same time as your estimated tax payment, and update the application if the full-year forecast has moved.

A job offer with health coverage changes things too. HealthCare.gov notes that once you have an offer of affordable job-based coverage, you usually lose eligibility for the credit "whether you enroll in the job-based coverage or not." For 2027, employer coverage counts as affordable if your share is no more than 10.22% of household income (Rev. Proc. 2026-26).

At tax time: Form 1095-A and Form 8962

  1. Wait for Form 1095-A. It comes from the Marketplace, not the IRS, "in the mail by mid-February," and HealthCare.gov enrollees may find it in their account "as early as mid-January" (HealthCare.gov). State Marketplaces send their own. It shows the months covered, monthly premiums, the second lowest cost Silver plan (SLCSP) premium and the advance payments made.
  2. Check it. Confirm the people, the coverage months and Part III, column B (the SLCSP premium). That column is wrong if it shows 0 or is blank for a covered month, or if you had household changes you didn't report; HealthCare.gov has a tax tool to get the right figure. Wrong name, address or SSN doesn't need a corrected form. If you get a "Corrected" 1095-A, use it; a "Voided" one means don't use it.
  3. File Form 8962 with your return. If any advance credit was paid, you must file and reconcile "even if you're usually not required to file," and skipping it can delay your refund and affect future advance payments (IRS).
  4. Pay what's due. Excess advance credit goes on Schedule 2. If you know a repayment is coming, add it to your tax set-aside; it isn't covered by your estimated payments unless you sized them for it.

Note: the 2026 Form 8962 instructions are still an IRS draft (dated Oct. 20, 2026); we'll recheck this page when the final version is posted. The 2025 final instructions still describe the old repayment caps because they apply to tax year 2025.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Entering gross receipts instead of net profit. The Marketplace wants profit after business expenses.
  • Using last year's income by default. The credit is based on the coverage year. A slow year last year doesn't help if this year is better.
  • Ignoring the 400% line. Under the new rules there's no partial credit above it and no cap on repayment.
  • Not updating mid-year. The repayment grows every month advance payments stay too high.
  • Filing before the 1095-A is right. A corrected form can mean an amended return.
  • Forgetting the spouse's offer of coverage. If a spouse's job offers family coverage, you usually don't qualify for the credit.

FAQ

Can freelancers get Marketplace health insurance?

Yes. HealthCare.gov says you can enroll through the individual Marketplace if you're a freelancer, consultant, independent contractor or other self-employed worker without employees. Savings are based on your estimated household income for the coverage year, not last year's income.

What income do I put on the Marketplace application if I'm self-employed?

Your expected net self-employment income for the coverage year, meaning business income minus the expenses you'd deduct on Schedule C, plus other household income. The Marketplace uses modified adjusted gross income: AGI plus untaxed foreign income, nontaxable Social Security benefits and tax-exempt interest. If you're asked to prove it, a self-employment ledger (a spreadsheet or accounting-software report) is accepted.

What happens if I earn more than I estimated?

When you file, Form 8962 compares the credit you qualify for on your actual income with the advance payments made for you. For tax years after 2025 there's no repayment cap, so the whole excess is added to your tax or taken from your refund. If your household income ends up above 400% of the poverty line, you're not eligible at all and generally repay all the advance credit.

What is the 400% limit for 2027 coverage?

For 2027 coverage, eligibility uses the 2026 poverty guidelines: 400% is $63,840 for a single person and $132,000 for a family of four in the 48 contiguous states and D.C. (higher in Alaska and Hawaii). For 2026 coverage, which you reconcile on your 2026 return, it's $62,600 single and $128,600 for a family of four.

When will I get Form 1095-A, and what if it's wrong?

HealthCare.gov says Form 1095-A comes from the Marketplace, not the IRS, by mail by mid-February, and may be in your HealthCare.gov account as early as mid-January (state Marketplaces send their own). Don't file until it's accurate. If the coverage months, people or second lowest cost Silver plan premium are wrong, contact the Marketplace for a corrected form; a wrong name or address doesn't need one.

Can a SEP or solo 401(k) contribution lower my household income?

On your tax return, household income starts from AGI, so deductible contributions to a SEP or a traditional (pre-tax) solo 401(k) lower it, just as they lower income tax. SEP contributions for a year can be made up to your return's due date including extensions, per the IRS SEP FAQs. Roth contributions don't lower AGI. Check the effect before filing, because near the 400% line a few thousand dollars can decide whether you keep any credit.
