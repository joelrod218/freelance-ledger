Freelance Contract Template: What to Include (With a Free Copyable Outline)

By Freelance Ledger · Published November 23, 2026 · How we research

Not legal advice. This is general information and a starting outline, not legal advice, and using it doesn’t create a lawyer-client relationship. Contract law varies by state and country. For large projects, valuable intellectual property, or a client’s own lengthy contract, have a lawyer licensed in your state review it.

Short answer: a freelance contract should say who the parties are, exactly what you will deliver (and what you won’t), by when, how much and when you get paid, what happens if the client pays late or cancels, how many revisions are included, and who owns the work once it’s paid for. Those clauses prevent most freelance disputes. Below are all 14 with sample wording, a copyable outline, a worked payment schedule, and the two state laws (New York and Illinois) that require some of these terms in writing.

The 14 clauses at a glance

ClauseWhat it prevents

What to include, clause by clause

1. Parties and contact details

Your legal name (and business name if you use one), the client’s legal entity name, both mailing addresses, emails, and who on the client side approves work and invoices.

Sample wording: This Agreement is between [Your legal name], doing business as [Business name], [mailing address], [email] (“Freelancer”), and [Client legal name], [mailing address] (“Client”). Client’s contact for approvals and invoices is [name, email].

2. Scope of work and deliverables

The single most important clause. List each deliverable, its format and quantity, and say what is not included. Vague scope is where unpaid extra work comes from.

Sample wording: Freelancer will deliver: [e.g. a 5-page website: home, about, services, blog template, contact; responsive; built on (platform)]. Not included: [copywriting, stock photo licenses, hosting, ongoing maintenance].

3. Timeline and client responsibilities

Start date, milestones, delivery date, and what the client must provide (content, logins, feedback) and by when. Make deadlines move if the client is late.

Sample wording: Work starts [date]. Milestones: [draft by date], [final by date]. Client will provide [content, access] by [date] and give feedback within [5] business days. If Client is late, delivery dates move by the same number of days.

4. Fees

A fixed project price, or an hourly/day rate with an estimate and a cap you must approve before exceeding. Name the currency if the client is abroad.

Sample wording: Project fee: [$4,800 USD], fixed for the scope above. Work outside the scope is billed at [$75]/hour, only after Client approves an estimate in writing.

5. Payment schedule, method and due dates

The deposit, milestone payments, how the client pays (bank transfer, card, check), and the exact due date or rule (“within 15 days of invoice”). If your client needs invoices by a cutoff date to pay on time, put that date here too.

Sample wording: [50%] deposit due on signing; [25%] on approval of [milestone]; [25%] on final delivery. Invoices are due within [15] days by [ACH/bank transfer/card]. Freelancer will submit invoices by [date or rule] to meet Client’s payment processing deadlines.

6. Expenses

Which costs the client reimburses (fonts, stock images, travel, software licenses bought for the project) and whether they need pre-approval.

Sample wording: Client will reimburse pre-approved project expenses at cost, such as [stock images, fonts, travel], invoiced with receipts.

7. Late payment

A clear consequence: a late fee or interest (check what your state allows), pausing work, and holding final files until paid.

Sample wording: Invoices unpaid [15] days after the due date incur a late fee of [x]% per month on the unpaid balance, where permitted by law. Freelancer may pause work while any invoice is overdue.

8. Revisions and change requests

How many rounds of revisions are included, how fast the client must request them, and how extra rounds or new requests are priced.

Sample wording: The fee includes [2] rounds of revisions requested within [5] business days of each delivery. Additional rounds or changes to the scope are billed at [$75]/hour or quoted separately.

9. Ownership and usage rights

Who owns the finished work, when ownership or a license passes (most freelancers tie it to full payment), what you keep (your tools, templates, pre-existing material), and whether you can show the work in your portfolio.

Sample wording: On receipt of full payment, Freelancer assigns to Client all copyright in the final deliverables [or: grants Client a (non-exclusive / exclusive) license to use them for (purpose)]. Freelancer keeps ownership of pre-existing materials, tools and drafts not selected, and may display the final work in its portfolio [unless Client objects in writing].

10. Confidentiality

Both sides keep the other’s non-public information private and use it only for the project.

Sample wording: Each party will keep the other’s confidential information private and use it only to perform this Agreement.

11. Independent contractor relationship

States that you are not an employee: you control how and when you work, use your own equipment, can work for others, and handle your own taxes. (The label alone doesn’t decide tax status; see below.)

Sample wording: Freelancer is an independent contractor, not an employee, partner or agent of Client. Freelancer controls the manner and means of the work, provides its own equipment, may serve other clients, and is responsible for its own taxes.

12. Cancellation and termination

How either side can end the project, the notice period, and what you are paid: work done to date plus a kill fee or a non-refundable deposit.

Sample wording: Either party may end this Agreement with [10] business days’ written notice. Client will pay for all work completed to the termination date and approved expenses; the deposit is non-refundable [and a kill fee of (x)% of the remaining fee applies].

13. Limitation of liability and warranties

Caps what you can owe if something goes wrong (often the fee paid) and says you aren’t responsible for content the client supplies.

Sample wording: Each party’s total liability under this Agreement is limited to the fees paid under it. Client is responsible for the rights to materials it provides.

14. Disputes, governing law and signatures

Which state’s law applies, how disputes are handled (talk first, then mediation, small claims or court), that this document is the whole agreement, that changes must be in writing, and signature lines with dates.

Sample wording: This Agreement is governed by the laws of [state]. The parties will try to resolve disputes in good faith before [mediation / small claims court in (county)]. This is the entire agreement; changes must be in writing and signed by both parties. Signatures: [Freelancer, date] [Client, date].

Worked example: a $4,800 website project

A made-up project to show how the money clauses fit together.

WhenShareAmount
On signing (deposit, non-refundable)50%$2,400
Design approved (milestone)25%$1,200
Final delivery25%$1,200
Total100%$4,800
  • The client asks for a blog section that wasn’t in the scope. Because the contract lists deliverables and an hourly rate for extras, you quote 4 hours × $75 = $300, the client approves by email, and you add it to the final invoice.
  • The client cancels after approving the design. With the cancellation clause above, you keep the $2,400 deposit and the $1,200 milestone already earned ($3,600 total) and invoice for any further work done to the termination date.
  • The final $1,200 invoice is late. The late-payment clause lets you send a firm, specific reminder; our late payment email templates and unpaid invoice follow-up plan walk through each stage.

Without a deposit, all $4,800 would be at risk until the end. Taking money up front is also a simple test of whether the client is ready to pay.

States that require written freelance contracts

New York State: Freelance Isn’t Free Act

Since August 28, 2024, New York’s Freelance Isn’t Free Act (General Business Law Article 44-A) has covered freelance workers hired as independent contractors for $800 or more, “either by itself or when aggregated with all contracts for services between the same hiring party and freelance worker during the immediately preceding one hundred twenty days” (§ 1410). A freelance worker can be one person or a one-person company, “whether or not incorporated.” Sales representatives, lawyers, licensed medical professionals and construction contractors are excluded, as are government hiring parties.

The contract “shall be reduced to writing,” the hiring party must give you a copy, and it must include at least (§ 1412):

  • the name and mailing address of both parties;
  • an itemization of all services, their value, and the rate and method of compensation;
  • the date you must be paid, or how that date will be determined; and
  • the date by which you must submit a list of services rendered so the client can meet its internal payment deadlines.

If the contract doesn’t set a payment date, payment is due no later than 30 days after you complete the work, and once you’ve started, the client can’t make timely payment conditional on you accepting less than the agreed amount (§ 1411). The Department of Labor publishes that free model contract, and complaints go to the New York Attorney General.

Illinois: Freelance Worker Protection Act

Illinois’s law covers independent contractors who provide products or services in Illinois or for a hiring entity located in Illinois when the work is worth $500 or more in a 120-day period, for contracts taking effect after July 1, 2024 (Illinois Department of Labor). The written contract must include the name and contact information of both parties, an itemization of products and services, the rate and method of compensation, and the date compensation is due; if no due date is set, full payment is due within 30 days of completing the work. The department posts free sample contracts in several languages on the same page, and its FAQ notes a contract can be “a paper document, an email, text message, or some other communication” both sides agreed to.

Some cities have their own freelance-payment rules too, so search your city and state plus “freelance worker protection” before relying on this list. Even where no law requires it, the same four items are a sensible minimum for any client.

Who owns the work: the copyright default

Under U.S. law, “ordinarily, the author is the person or persons who actually created the work” (Copyright Office Circular 30). A freelancer’s work becomes a “work made for hire” owned by the client only if all four of these are true: it falls in one of nine categories listed in the Copyright Act (such as a contribution to a collective work, part of a film or audiovisual work, a translation or an instructional text), there’s a written agreement, the agreement expressly says it’s a work made for hire, and both parties sign it. “If a work fails to satisfy any of these requirements, it is not a work made for hire.”

Outside those categories, the client gets ownership only through a transfer, and “a transfer of copyright ownership… is not valid unless an instrument of conveyance, or a note or memorandum of the transfer, is in writing and signed by the owner of the rights conveyed” (17 U.S.C. 204(a)). In practice: write down whether you are assigning ownership or granting a license, for what uses, and that it happens on full payment. Clients often send “work made for hire” language by default; read it, because it can also cover drafts, tools and templates you want to keep.

Contracts and your tax status

An “independent contractor” clause helps show what both sides intended, but it doesn’t settle your tax status. The IRS’s general rule is that you’re an independent contractor if the client “has the right to control or direct only the result of the work and not what will be done and how it will be done.” When classification is in question, the IRS says to look at the entire relationship: “no one factor stands alone in making this determination.” As a contractor you’re self-employed, so you’ll pay self-employment tax and usually quarterly estimated taxes. Expect U.S. clients to ask for a Form W-9 before the first payment.

Sending and signing it

  • E-signatures are fine for most work. Under the ESIGN Act, a signature or contract for a transaction in or affecting interstate or foreign commerce “may not be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because it is in electronic form.” A signed PDF or an e-signature tool both work.
  • Keep the signed copy with your other business records (see how to track income and expenses).
  • Use a tool if you send contracts often. Bonsai, HoneyBook and Dubsado bundle contract templates, e-signing and deposit invoices; we compare prices and fees in best contract and proposal software and Bonsai vs HoneyBook.
  • Bill against it. Make each invoice match the payment schedule and quote the due date; our free invoice generator works without signup. For clients abroad, name the currency and who pays transfer fees (getting paid by international clients).

Common mistakes

  • “Website redesign” as the whole scope. List pages, deliverables and exclusions.
  • No deposit. You carry all the risk until the end.
  • Unlimited revisions. Put a number on it and price extra rounds.
  • Handing over ownership on signing. Tie the transfer to full payment.
  • Signing the client’s template without reading it. Look for payment terms of net 60 or longer, broad work-for-hire and non-compete clauses, and unlimited liability or indemnity.
  • Not setting your rate first. Work it out with our freelance hourly rate calculator before you quote.

FAQ

Is this freelance contract template legally binding?

A contract is generally binding when both sides agree to the terms, but whether a particular clause holds up depends on your state's law and the facts. This outline is a starting point, not legal advice. For a large project, a client with its own long contract, or anything involving liability or ownership of valuable work, have a lawyer licensed in your state review it.

Do I need a written contract for freelance work?

In most places a written contract is not required for every job, but it is the best evidence of what was agreed. Some laws do require one: New York State's Freelance Isn't Free Act requires a written contract for freelance work worth $800 or more (alone or combined with the same client over 120 days), and Illinois's Freelance Worker Protection Act requires one at $500 or more over 120 days.

Can a contract be signed electronically?

Generally yes. The federal ESIGN Act (15 U.S.C. 7001) says a signature or contract relating to a transaction in or affecting interstate or foreign commerce may not be denied legal effect solely because it is in electronic form. Keep a downloaded copy of the signed version.

Who owns the work if the contract says nothing?

Under U.S. copyright law the person who creates a work is ordinarily its author and owner. Work by a freelancer is a 'work made for hire' owned by the client only if it falls in one of nine categories listed in the Copyright Act and both parties sign a written agreement saying so, and any transfer of copyright must be in a writing signed by the owner. So spell out ownership and usage rights in the contract.

Does a contract make me an independent contractor for tax purposes?

Not by itself. The IRS looks at the entire relationship, including behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship, and says no one factor stands alone. The general rule is that you are an independent contractor if the client controls only the result of the work, not what will be done and how.

What should I do if a client won't sign a contract?

At minimum, send an email that lists the scope, price, payment date and deliverables and ask the client to reply confirming. Illinois's Department of Labor notes a contract can be an email, text message or other communication both sides agreed to. Then invoice promptly and follow up on late payments in writing.
