If you drive to clients, shoots, job sites or the post office for your business, every forgotten trip is money left on the table. In the second half of 2026 the IRS business rate is 76 cents a mile, so 100 forgotten miles is a $76 deduction you can't claim. Mileage apps fix the forgetting: they run on your phone, detect drives and build the log the IRS expects. The catch is that free plans cap trips, and prices range from $0 to over $200 a year. Here's how the main options compare for a one-person business, with prices checked today on each app's own site.

Is a paid app worth it? Quick math

The 2026 IRS business standard mileage rate is 72.5 cents a mile for January 1 to June 30 and 76 cents from July 1 to December 31 (IRS). A freelancer who drives 3,000 business miles in each half of 2026 has a deduction of 3,000 × $0.725 + 3,000 × $0.76 = $4,455. Our mileage deduction calculator runs this for your own miles.

The break-even question is how many trips you'd otherwise forget. MileIQ's annual plan ($139.92) is covered by the deduction on about 185 extra business miles at 76 cents (185 × $0.76 = $140.60). TripLog Premium ($59.99) is covered by about 79 miles. Remember that a deduction isn't cash back: what you actually save is the deduction times your tax rate (income tax plus self-employment tax). If you drive for business only a few times a month, a free plan or a simple written log is enough.

The apps, one by one

1. TripLog: best value

TripLog's free Basic plan includes unlimited automatic GPS mileage tracking and basic expense management. Premium is $4.99 a month, paid annually as $59.99, and adds unlimited reporting, automatic expense tracking and integrations, per TripLog's pricing page. On the free plan, unlimited reporting comes with a 7-day pass: TripLog gives one free Premium pass per calendar year, available from January 1, which is enough to download your annual mileage report for tax time. It also sells optional hardware (TripLog Beacon $19.99, TripLog Drive $79.99) if you'd rather not rely on phone motion detection.

Pick it if you drive regularly and want the lowest paid price, or want free tracking with no trip cap and are fine pulling one report a year.

2. Everlance: mileage plus expenses and tax filing

Everlance Basic is free with 30 automatically tracked trips a month and unlimited manual trips. Starter ($89.99 a year or $10.99 a month) unlocks unlimited automatic tracking, expense tracking with receipt uploads and IRS-compliant reports. Professional ($119.99 a year or $19.99 a month) adds automatic expense tracking, an AI deduction finder, all-inclusive 1099 tax filing and $1 million audit defense, per Everlance's pricing page. New users get a 7-day free trial of Starter, which needs a credit card and converts to a paid plan unless you cancel, and Everlance says sales are non-refundable.

Pick it if you want mileage and expenses in one place, or want filing bundled in at a low price. Check what the filing includes for your situation before relying on it.

3. MileIQ: simplest day-to-day

MileIQ tracks drives automatically and you swipe left or right to mark each one business or personal. The free plan covers 40 drives a month. Unlimited costs $11.66 a month billed annually ($139.92 a year) or $13.99 month to month, and includes auto-generated reports.

Pick it if you want the least effort and drive often enough that 40 drives a month isn't enough. It's mileage only, so you'll still need something for expenses.

4. Driversnote: tidy reports, beacon option

Driversnote works on iOS, Android and the web. The free plan tracks automatically but lets you report up to 15 trips a month. Pro is $11 a month ($132 a year in Driversnote's own listing), excluding sales tax, with unlimited trips and reporting; a yearly subscription includes a free iBeacon (a $40 value) that sits in your car so only drives in that car are tracked. Driversnote also says it has partnered with Wave in the US and Canada, handy if you keep your books in Wave.

Pick it if you share or switch vehicles and want the beacon to keep personal rides (carpools, rideshares) out of your log.

5. Stride: free

Stride is a free app for independent workers that tracks mileage, income and expenses and produces IRS-ready expense reports you can hand to an accountant or upload to tax software. It doesn't file your taxes. Stride's other big product is health insurance: it's an official HealthCare.gov partner that helps independent workers compare and enroll in plans, which is worth a look during open enrollment.

Pick it if you want to spend nothing and your needs are simple.

6. QuickBooks Solopreneur: if you need accounting too

QuickBooks Solopreneur uses your phone's GPS to track miles, alongside receipt capture, invoicing and Schedule C-oriented bookkeeping. It's $20 a month, or $10 a month for the first 3 months for new customers (QuickBooks pricing). The QuickBooks Free plan includes mileage tracking for 5 trips a month; Solopreneur unlocks unlimited receipts and mileage. It costs more than a mileage-only app, but if you'd pay for accounting software anyway, it saves you a second subscription. What your mileage log must show

IRS Publication 463 (chapter 5) lists what you need to prove car expenses. For each business use:

  • Miles: the mileage for each business use, plus the total miles you drove the car during the year.
  • Date of the trip.
  • Destination (your business destination).
  • Business purpose, such as "client meeting, Acme Co." If the purpose is obvious from the circumstances, you don't need a written explanation.

Pub 463 says you "can't deduct amounts that you approximate or estimate." A record prepared on a computer is considered adequate, so an app log is fine. It should be timely, recorded at or near the time of the trip; a log kept weekly that covers that week's use counts. A round trip or an uninterrupted run of business stops can be one record, and a minimal personal stop such as lunch between two business stops doesn't break it. Keep the records for at least 3 years from the date you file the return (longer for a car you depreciate).

Commuting doesn't count. Driving between your home and your main or regular place of work is personal commuting, however far it is. If your home office is your principal place of business, trips from home to clients and other work locations can qualify; Pub 463 has the details. Whichever app you choose, set work hours or classify each trip so personal drives stay out of the business total.

Standard mileage rate vs actual expenses

An app gives you the miles; you still choose how to deduct. Per IRS Topic 510:

  • Standard mileage rate: business miles × the IRS rate. It covers the cost of operating the car, so you don't also deduct gas, insurance or repairs. Business parking and tolls are deductible on top.
  • Actual expenses: the business-use share of gas, oil, repairs, tires, insurance, registration, licenses, and depreciation or lease payments. You still need the mileage log to work out the business share.
  • Choose early. To use the standard rate for a car you own, you must choose it in the first year the car is available for use in your business; in later years you can switch between methods. For a leased car, choosing the standard rate locks it in for the whole lease. Other limits apply (for example, no standard rate if you run five or more cars at once).
  • Where it goes: self-employed car expenses go on Schedule C.

FAQ

What is the best free mileage tracking app for freelancers?

It depends on how much you drive. As of November 4, 2026, Stride is free with mileage and expense tracking, TripLog's free Basic plan includes unlimited automatic GPS tracking (unlimited reports need Premium or its once-a-year 7-day pass), MileIQ's free plan covers 40 drives a month, Everlance's free plan covers 30 automatically tracked trips a month plus unlimited manual trips, and Driversnote's free plan lets you report up to 15 trips a month. If you drive for work most days, the free limits run out quickly.

What is the IRS mileage rate for 2026?

For business driving in 2026 the IRS standard mileage rate is 72.5 cents a mile for January 1 to June 30 and 76 cents a mile from July 1 to December 31. The 2027 rate had not been announced as of November 4, 2026.

Does the IRS accept a mileage log from an app?

Yes. IRS Publication 463 says a record prepared on a computer is considered an adequate record. What matters is the content: the miles for each business use, the date, the destination and the business purpose, plus total miles for the year, recorded at or near the time. A log kept weekly counts as timely.

Can I deduct gas as well as mileage?

Not if you use the standard mileage rate. The rate replaces gas, oil, repairs, insurance and depreciation. You can deduct business parking fees and tolls on top of it. The other choice is the actual expense method, where you deduct the business share of your real car costs instead.

Is a mileage tracking app tax-deductible?

A subscription you use for your freelance business is generally a deductible business expense on Schedule C, because ordinary and necessary business expenses are deductible. If you also use it for personal driving records, only the business share counts.
