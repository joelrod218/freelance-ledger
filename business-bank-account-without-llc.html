Business Bank Account Without an LLC: Do Sole Proprietors Need One, and Where Can You Open It?

If you freelance under your own name, you're a sole proprietor by default. No LLC, no paperwork with the state. Two questions follow: do I actually need a business bank account? and can I even open one without an LLC or an EIN? Short answers: it's not legally required, but it's the cheapest bookkeeping upgrade you can make. And yes, most online business accounts take sole proprietors with nothing more than a Social Security number. One popular option doesn't. If you already know you want an account and just want the feature comparison, see our best business bank accounts for freelancers.

The short version

  • Not required by law. A sole proprietorship "has no existence apart from you," per the IRS. No federal rule says its money has to sit in a business-titled account.
  • Still recommended by the IRS. Publication 583 says one of the first things you should do is open a business checking account and keep it separate from your personal one.
  • No LLC or EIN needed at most banks. Found, Lili, Relay, Bluevine and Chase take sole proprietors with an SSN. Novo wants an EIN. Mercury doesn't take sole proprietors at all.
  • A DBA matters for your name. Without one, the account is usually opened in your legal name. You need a registered trade name (DBA) to use a business name and, at some banks, to deposit checks made out to it.
  • FDIC coverage is combined. A sole-proprietor account is insured together with your personal single accounts at the same bank, up to $250,000 total.
  • It doesn't protect your personal assets. A separate account organizes your money. It isn't a liability shield.

Do you need a business bank account as a sole proprietor?

Legally, no. The IRS sole proprietorships page defines a sole proprietor as someone who owns an unincorporated business by themselves, and Publication 583 says the business's liabilities are your personal liabilities and its income and expenses go on your personal return. On records, Pub 583 says that "except in a few cases, the law does not require any specific kind of records." You can choose any system that clearly shows your income and expenses.

Practically, a separate account is how most freelancers meet that standard without pain. Publication 583:

  • says to open a business checking account early, keep it separate from your personal account, and "use the business account for business purposes only";
  • calls the business checkbook "the main source for entries in the business books" for most small businesses;
  • says to deposit all business receipts into it, and to write checks to yourself only when you withdraw money for personal use (that's your owner's draw; sole proprietors don't pay themselves a salary);
  • says your records must support the income and deductions on your return, and that bank statements can help prove a payment (though proof of payment alone doesn't prove the expense was deductible; keep the receipt or invoice too).

Here's the real cost of mixing: if every client payment, grocery run and software subscription runs through one account, your Schedule C becomes an annual archaeology project, and in an exam you'd be sorting personal from business deposits line by line. With a separate account, the statement is most of your bookkeeping.

When a second personal account is enough, and when it isn't

For tax purposes, what matters is separation and records, not the word "business" on the account. A dedicated second checking account in your own name does the separating job. A business-titled account adds a few practical things: clients can pay a business name, you can deposit checks made out to that name (Novo, for example, says you need a DBA for that), and you get business tools like invoicing and payment links. Because the free business accounts below cost $0 a month, there's little reason not to use one.

SSN or EIN: should you get one anyway?

Per the IRS employer ID numbers page, you need an EIN if you have employees, pay employment, excise or alcohol, tobacco and firearms taxes, or withhold tax on non-wage payments to a non-resident alien, or if you operate as a partnership, LLC, corporation or certain other entities. A solo freelancer with none of those doesn't need one for federal taxes. But the same page says that if you don't need an EIN for federal tax purposes, "you can still request one for banking or state tax purposes."

Reasons to get one anyway:

  • It's free and fast. The IRS online EIN application issues the number immediately if approved and warns that you never have to pay a fee for an EIN. Skip the sites that charge.
  • It keeps your SSN off client paperwork. A sole proprietor can put either an SSN or an EIN on a Form W-9.
  • Novo requires one, and some traditional banks may too.
  • You only ever need one. The Form SS-4 instructions say a sole proprietor generally needs only one EIN, however many businesses or trade names they run. If you later incorporate or take on a partner, that new entity needs its own.

Business name: do you need a DBA?

Only if you want to work under a name other than your own. Found, Novo and Bluevine all say a sole-proprietor account is opened under your legal name unless you have a registered DBA. The SBA business name guide says you might need to register your DBA (also called a trade name, fictitious name or assumed name) with your state, county or city, and that most states require registration if you use one. A DBA gives you no legal protection by itself. Novo's help center puts the filing fee at usually $10 to $100. Check your own state or county for the actual rules and cost.

How to open one without an LLC

  1. Decide on the name. Your own name (nothing to file) or a DBA (register it first if you want checks and invoices in that name).
  2. Optional: get a free EIN on IRS.gov. Required only at Novo among the providers above.
  3. Have these ready: government photo ID, SSN, date of birth, and a physical U.S. address. Relay and Bluevine don't accept P.O. boxes, and Relay doesn't accept virtual mailboxes. You'll also need your DBA certificate if you have one and a short description of what you do.
  4. Apply online and choose "sole proprietorship" as the business type. Don't pick LLC if you haven't formed one.
  5. Switch your money flow. Update your invoices, payment links and client payment details so every client payment lands in the new account. Move business subscriptions to its debit card.
  6. Pay yourself and your taxes from it. Transfer a set amount to personal checking as an owner's draw, and move a percentage of every payment into a tax savings bucket. Our tax set-aside calculator gives you the percentage, and the quarterly estimated taxes guide covers when to pay.

FDIC insurance works differently for sole proprietors

This one surprises people. Per the FDIC's Your Insured Deposits, an account held in the name of a sole proprietorship, including a DBA account, falls in the Single Account category. The FDIC "insures deposits owned by a sole proprietorship as a Single Account of the business owner" and adds it to your other single accounts at the same bank, up to $250,000 in total. LLC and corporation accounts, by contrast, are insured separately from the owners' personal deposits.

Two practical notes for fintech accounts. First, FDIC coverage protects you if the partner bank fails, not the app company. What a separate account doesn't do

It doesn't separate you from the business. The IRS says a sole proprietorship's liabilities are your personal liabilities, and the SBA says a sole proprietorship doesn't produce a separate business entity, so you can be held personally liable for business debts. A business checking account keeps the books clean. It doesn't put a wall around your savings or your house. If liability is the worry, look at insurance and business structure. FAQ

Do I need a business bank account as a sole proprietor?

No federal law requires one. A sole proprietorship has no existence apart from you, and the IRS says that except in a few cases the law doesn't require any specific kind of records. But IRS Publication 583 says one of the first things you should do when you start a business is open a business checking account and keep it separate from your personal account, because it becomes the main source for your books and backs up the income and deductions on your Schedule C.

Can I open a business bank account without an LLC?

Yes. As of November 2, 2026, Found, Lili, Relay, Bluevine and Chase all list sole proprietorships as eligible. Novo accepts sole proprietors but its help center says you need an EIN. Mercury is the exception: its help center says it can't open accounts for sole proprietorships because there's no state formation document to verify.

Do I need an EIN to open a business bank account as a sole proprietor?

Usually not. Found and Lili say sole proprietors apply with their SSN, Relay says sole proprietors provide an SSN instead of an EIN, Bluevine lets you use your SSN if you don't have an EIN, and Chase accepts a Social Security number, TIN or EIN. Novo is the exception that asks sole proprietors for an EIN. An EIN is free from the IRS, and the IRS says you can request one for banking purposes even if you don't need one for taxes.

Is my sole proprietor business account FDIC-insured separately from my personal account?

No. The FDIC insures a sole proprietorship's deposits as Single Account deposits of the owner. It adds them to your other single accounts at the same bank and insures the total up to $250,000. If you have $180,000 in personal savings and $90,000 in a sole-proprietor business account at the same bank, $20,000 is uninsured. LLC and corporation accounts are insured separately from the owners' personal deposits.

Does a separate business bank account protect my personal assets?

Not for a sole proprietor. The IRS says the business's liabilities are your personal liabilities, and the SBA says a sole proprietorship doesn't create a separate business entity. A separate account keeps your records clean, but it doesn't create a legal shield. That takes a different business structure, which is a separate decision.
