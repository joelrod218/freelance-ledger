How self-employment tax is calculated for 2026

This is the same math as the 2026 Self-Employment Tax and Deduction Worksheet in Form 1040-ES:

  1. Take 92.35% of your net profit. That's your net earnings from self-employment. The 7.65% you skip mirrors the employer's half of payroll tax.
  2. Medicare: 2.9% of that whole amount. There's no cap.
  3. Social Security: 12.4%, but only up to the 2026 limit of $184,500, and the limit is shared with any W-2 wages. If your wages already reach $184,500, you pay no Social Security part on your freelance income.
  4. Add them. Together that's the familiar 15.3% rate (12.4% + 2.9%) on income under the cap.
  5. Deduct half. You can deduct 50% of your self-employment tax when you figure adjusted gross income (Schedule 1, line 15). That lowers your income tax, not the self-employment tax itself.

If your net earnings from self-employment (step 1) are under $400, Schedule SE says you don't owe self-employment tax.

The 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax

High earners owe an extra 0.9% Medicare tax on wages and self-employment income above a threshold set by filing status: $200,000 single, head of household or qualifying surviving spouse; $250,000 married filing jointly; $125,000 married filing separately. According to the IRS, wages and self-employment income are combined to test the threshold: your threshold is first reduced by your Medicare wages (not below zero), and the 0.9% applies to self-employment income above what's left. It's figured on Form 8959, separately from Schedule SE, so it isn't part of the self-employment tax you deduct half of. There's no employer match for it.

The calculator shows the Additional Medicare Tax on your self-employment income. If your own wages are over your threshold, you also owe 0.9% on that excess wage amount; an employer withholds it only on wages above $200,000.

How to pay it

There's no separate bill for self-employment tax. You pay it through quarterly estimated tax payments along with your income tax, and settle up on your return with Schedule SE. The next 2026 payment is due January 15, 2027. Dividing the calculator's total by four gives you the self-employment tax piece of each quarterly payment; add your expected income tax to get the full amount.

Ways to lower it (legally)

  • Track every business expense. Self-employment tax is based on profit, so while you're under the Social Security limit, each $100 of deductible expenses cuts it by about $14.13 (15.3% × 92.35%), on top of the income tax savings.
  • Don't miss the half-tax deduction. It's claimed automatically by tax software, but if you estimate by hand, subtract it before figuring income tax.

FAQ

What is the self-employment tax rate for 2026?

15.3%: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare, applied to 92.35% of your net profit. The Social Security part stops at $184,500 of combined wages and self-employment earnings in 2026. An extra 0.9% Medicare tax applies above $200,000 (single), $250,000 (married filing jointly) or $125,000 (married filing separately).

Why is it 92.35% and not my full profit?

The IRS has you multiply net profit by 92.35% (0.9235) on Schedule SE and in the Form 1040-ES worksheet. The result, your net earnings from self-employment, is what the 15.3% applies to.

Do I owe self-employment tax if I made very little?

Not if your net earnings from self-employment are under $400 for the year. You still report the income for income tax purposes.

I have a full-time job too. Does that change anything?

Yes. Your W-2 wages count toward the same $184,500 Social Security limit, so a high salary can reduce or eliminate the Social Security part on your side income. The 2.9% Medicare part still applies to all of your self-employment earnings. You can also cover the tax by raising your W-4 withholding instead of sending quarterly payments.

What's the Social Security limit for 2027?

The SSA's contribution and benefit base table lists $192,300 for 2027, up from $184,500 for 2026. Use it when you start planning 2027 estimated payments.

Is self-employment tax the same as income tax?

No. It's a separate tax for Social Security and Medicare, owed in addition to federal income tax (and any state tax). Both are paid through the same estimated payments and the same return.
