Freelance photographer tax deductions (2026)

By Freelance Ledger · Last reviewed October 22, 2026 · How we research

Checked against IRS sources Oct 22, 2026: Publication 334, Publication 587, Publication 946 and Publication 463 (2025 editions, the latest the IRS has posted, plus their "What's New for 2026" notes), Publication 538 (current January 2022 revision), the 2025 Schedule C instructions, Topic 511, Topic 513 and the 2026 standard mileage rates page. Tax rules change. This is general information, not tax advice.

If you shoot for clients as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC (weddings, portraits, events, products, real estate or stock), your business expenses go on Schedule C. IRS Publication 334 sets the test: an expense must be ordinary (common and accepted in your field) and necessary (helpful and appropriate for your business). This is the photographer cut of our general freelance tax deductions checklist; there are also writer, graphic designer and developer versions.

The short answer

Freelance photographers usually write off camera bodies, lenses, lighting and other gear (with extra record rules if the gear is also used personally), editing software and cloud storage, studio rent, business insurance, prints and albums sold to clients (as cost of goods sold), second shooters and retouchers, marketing and a portfolio site, travel to shoots away from home, a home office when the rules are met, and the business share of phone and internet. Business meals are generally 50% deductible. Commuting is not deductible.

Cameras, lenses, lighting and drones

Gear is usually a photographer's biggest deduction. There are three common ways to write it off. Whichever you use, deduct only the business-use share.

  • De minimis safe harbor: you can elect to deduct tangible property costing up to $2,500 per item or invoice if you don't have an applicable financial statement (most freelancers don't), per the IRS tangible property regulations page. That often covers a lens, flash, light stand kit, memory cards or a gimbal. You have to make the election on your return.
  • Section 179: lets you expense qualifying equipment in the year you start using it. The dollar caps ($2,500,000 for tax years beginning in 2025 and $2,560,000 for 2026, per Publication 946) are far above what a solo photographer spends; the deduction is also limited by your business income.
  • Depreciation / special depreciation allowance: for qualified property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, Publication 946 says the 100% special depreciation allowance (bonus depreciation) is back, so a new camera body can often be written off in year one.

The listed-property catch

The Schedule C instructions (line 13) say listed property includes property used for entertainment or recreation, such as photographic and video recording equipment. The exception: gear used exclusively in your business or at your regular business establishment isn't listed property (part of your home counts as a business establishment only if it meets the home office rules). Why it matters:

  • Publication 946 says that if listed property isn't used more than 50% for qualified business use, you can't claim Section 179 or the special depreciation allowance on it and must depreciate it straight-line over the longer ADS recovery period.
  • If business use later drops to 50% or less, you may have to recapture (add back to income) the excess depreciation.
  • Depreciation on listed property is reported on Form 4562 every year, and you need records of business vs personal use.

Practical habit: if your camera also goes on family trips, keep a simple log of business shoots vs personal use, or keep a dedicated work body.

Editing software, storage and computers

  • Subscriptions to editing and culling software, presets you buy for client work, plugins and cloud backup are ordinary business expenses.
  • Client galleries and booking tools: gallery hosting, contract and invoicing software, scheduling apps and payment-processing fees.
  • Software bought outright: Publication 946 says off-the-shelf software can qualify for Section 179; if you depreciate it instead, it is straight-line over 36 months.
  • Computers, monitors and drives follow the same de minimis / Section 179 / bonus routes as camera gear; Publication 946 treats computers and peripheral equipment as 5-year property.

Studio rent, insurance and other overhead

  • Studio or shared-space rent: Publication 334 says rent is deductible if it's for property you use in your business (and you're not getting equity or title).
  • Insurance: Publication 334 lists fire, theft, flood and similar coverage and liability insurance among deductible business premiums, which covers typical gear and liability policies for photographers.
  • Props, backdrops and consumables used up on shoots, batteries, memory cards and repairs.
  • Marketing: portfolio website and domain, ads, printed samples, bridal-show booths, and professional association dues.
  • Legal and accounting fees for contracts, model releases and bookkeeping.

Prints, albums and products you sell

If you sell prints, albums, canvases or framed work, what those products cost you is generally cost of goods sold (Schedule C Part III), not a regular expense line. Publication 334 says a small business taxpayer (average annual gross receipts of $31 million or less for the 3 prior tax years, and not a tax shelter) can treat inventory as non-incidental materials and supplies, deducting it in the year the items are provided to customers.

Photographers also get a break on the uniform capitalization (UNICAP) rules: the Schedule C instructions say freelance artists, authors and photographers may be exempt for property their own personal efforts create, but the exception does not apply to expenses for printing, photographic plates, motion picture films, videotapes or similar items. Small business taxpayers have a separate exception (see Publication 538). If you sell a lot of physical product, a preparer can confirm your method.

Second shooters, assistants and retouchers

Contractors you hire (second shooters, assistants, retouchers, editors, stylists) go on Schedule C line 11 (contract labor). The 2025 instructions use a $600 Form 1099-NEC threshold; Publication 334 says the information-reporting threshold rises to $2,000 for payments made after 2025. Collect a W-9 up front (see our W-9 guide).

Travel to shoots

Overnight trips away from your tax home for a destination wedding or an out-of-town job (airfare, lodging, baggage fees for gear, 50% of qualifying meals) follow Topic 511 and Publication 463. Topic 511 says a work assignment of more than one year is considered indefinite, and travel to it isn't deductible. Personal days tacked on to a trip are not deductible.

Car and mileage

Driving to client shoots, venues for walk-throughs and the camera shop counts; driving from home to a regular studio is commuting. For 2026, the IRS business rates are 72.5¢/mile from Jan 1–Jun 30 and 76¢/mile from Jul 1–Dec 31 (2025: 70¢) per the IRS standard mileage rates page. Our mileage deduction calculator does the math.

Home office and editing space

You need regular and exclusive use of part of your home as your principal place of business, or as a place where you meet clients (Publication 587). A spare room used only for editing and client consults qualifies; a dining table the family also uses generally fails the exclusive-use test. A qualifying home office also helps with the listed-property exception above.

The simplified option is $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet ($1,500 max), with no depreciation to track. The regular method uses actual expenses (rent or mortgage interest, utilities, insurance) on Form 8829. Compare both in our home office deduction calculator.

Phone and internet

Deduct the business-use share of your cell phone and home internet (uploading galleries counts). Write down how you estimated the percentage. The Schedule C instructions add one quirk: if you use a home landline for business, the base rate of the first phone line into your home isn't deductible, though extra business charges and a second line are.

Workshops and education

IRS Topic 513 allows self-employed people to deduct education that maintains or improves skills needed in their current work, such as a lighting workshop or an editing course for a working photographer. It doesn't cover education that's part of a program qualifying you for a new trade or business.

Meals and gifts

Business meals with a client or prospect are generally 50% deductible when they aren't lavish and you are present. Entertainment is generally not deductible. Business gifts (a thank-you for a referring planner, say) are limited to $25 per person per year (Publication 463).

Above-the-line items (not on Schedule C profit)

  • Half of self-employment tax as an adjustment to income. Estimate it with our self-employment tax calculator.
  • Self-employed health insurance premiums for you and your family, subject to profit and eligibility limits (Publication 334).
  • Retirement contributions to a Solo 401(k) or SEP IRA: see Solo 401(k) vs SEP IRA.
  • Qualified business income (QBI) deduction: up to 20% of qualified business income; Publication 334 notes the 20% QBI deduction was made permanent. Our quarterly tax calculator applies it for simple cases.

Not deductible

Commuting, the personal-use share of gear, entertainment, fines and penalties, and spending on photography as a hobby without a real profit motive. Repeated losses draw attention; Publication 334 covers profit motive and the excess-business-loss limits.

Records to keep

Keep receipts or invoices plus a one-line business purpose (the client or shoot name works well), the date you started using each piece of gear, and a business-use log for any gear you also use personally. The IRS record-keeping page says to keep records for 3 years in most cases, longer in some situations. FAQ

Can I write off a new camera body or lens in the year I buy it?

Often, yes. With the de minimis safe harbor election you can deduct items costing up to $2,500 per item or invoice if you don't have an applicable financial statement. Pricier gear can often be expensed with Section 179 or the 100% special depreciation allowance. Cameras can be listed property, though, so if you also use them personally you need more than 50% business use for those write-offs.

Is my camera gear listed property?

It can be. The Schedule C instructions say listed property includes photographic and video recording equipment, but not equipment used exclusively in your business or at your regular business establishment (a home office counts only if it meets the home office rules). If gear is listed property, keep a business-use log and report it on Form 4562.

Do I have to capitalize the costs of creating my photos?

Usually not for your own creative work. The Schedule C instructions say freelance artists, authors and photographers may be exempt from the uniform capitalization rules for property their own efforts create. The exception does not cover printing, photographic plates, films, videotapes or similar items. Small business taxpayers have a separate exception.

Can I deduct the cost of prints and albums I sell to clients?

Yes, but generally as cost of goods sold rather than as an ordinary expense. Publication 334 explains cost of goods sold (Schedule C Part III) and says a small business taxpayer with average annual gross receipts of $31 million or less can treat inventory as non-incidental materials and supplies, deducted in the year the items are provided to customers.

Do I need to file a 1099-NEC for a second shooter or retoucher?

Often. The 2025 Schedule C instructions say to file Form 1099-NEC for contract labor of $600 or more during the year. Publication 334 says the information-reporting threshold rises to $2,000 for payments made after 2025. Get a Form W-9 before you pay.
