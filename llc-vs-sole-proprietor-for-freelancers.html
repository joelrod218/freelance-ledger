LLC vs sole proprietor for freelancers

By Freelance Ledger · Last reviewed October 17, 2026 · How we research

General US information based on IRS guidance on LLCs (linked). State rules and fees vary a lot. Not legal or tax advice; for a real decision talk to a CPA or attorney.

If you freelance without forming anything, you are a sole proprietor by default. You can form a limited liability company (LLC) with your state. People often assume an LLC cuts their taxes; for most one-person businesses it doesn't. Here is what really changes.

SetupNone (a local business license or "doing business as" registration may apply)File with your state, pay its fee, usually keep an operating agreement
Federal income taxSchedule C on your personal returnSame by default (the IRS treats a single-member LLC as a "disregarded entity")

What an LLC can protect you from

An LLC can separate your personal assets from some business debts and lawsuits, for example a client's claim connected to a contract dispute with the business. It does not usually protect you from your own professional mistakes or from debts you personally guarantee, and the protection can be lost if you mix personal and business money. Keep a separate business bank account (see our bank account guide) and consider business insurance.

Taxes: what stays the same

A single-member LLC still reports profit on Schedule C and still pays self-employment tax, so the self-employment tax calculator and quarterly estimates work the same either way. The IRS explains the classification rules on its LLC page.

The S corporation election

An LLC can elect to be taxed as an S corporation (Form 2553). Some higher-earning freelancers do this to reduce self-employment tax on part of their profit, but it requires running payroll, paying yourself a "reasonable salary", filing a separate business return and often paying more for accounting. It usually only makes sense once profit is comfortably high. Ask a CPA to run the numbers for your state before electing.

How to decide

  • Stay a sole proprietor if your work is low-risk, your income is modest and you want the least paperwork.
  • Consider an LLC if you sign larger contracts, have physical risk (equipment, events), hire help or a client requires it.
  • Check your state's cost first. Annual fees range from small to several hundred dollars.

FAQ

Do I need an LLC to open a business bank account?

Not always. Many banks allow sole proprietors to open business accounts using their name or a "doing business as" registration. Requirements differ by bank.

Will an LLC lower my taxes?

Not by itself. A single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship unless you elect otherwise.

Do I need an EIN?

A sole proprietor without employees can usually use their SSN, but an EIN is free and keeps your SSN off W-9s. A multi-member LLC or one with employees needs one.
