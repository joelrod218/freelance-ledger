How to Track Freelance Income and Expenses (Spreadsheet vs Software)

Short answer: run every business payment through one separate account, record each transaction with a date, payee, amount and a category that matches a line on Schedule C, keep the receipt or invoice behind it, and spend about 15 minutes a week keeping it current. A free spreadsheet is enough if you have one business account and a few dozen transactions a month. Once you have several cards and accounts, regular driving, or you simply stop keeping the spreadsheet up, an app that imports your bank transactions saves time, at roughly $0 to $20 a month.

What the IRS actually requires

  • Any system that works. “You may choose any recordkeeping system suited to your business that clearly shows your income and expenses. Except in a few cases, the law does not require any special kind of records” (IRS recordkeeping).
  • A summary plus the documents behind it. Your books “must show your gross income, as well as your deductions and credits,” and supporting documents (“sales slips, paid bills, invoices, receipts, deposit slips, and canceled checks”) back up each entry. For expenses, the documents should identify “the payee, the amount paid, proof of payment, the date incurred,” and what it was for (IRS).
  • Electronic is fine. “All requirements that apply to hard copy books and records also apply to electronic records” (IRS). Photos of receipts in an app or a cloud folder count, as long as you can find and reproduce them legibly.
  • No guessing. For car, travel and gift expenses, “You can’t deduct amounts that you approximate or estimate,” and a record made “at or near the time of the expense or use” carries more weight than one prepared later (Publication 463). This is why a mileage log kept as you drive matters.
  • All income counts. You must report all business income, “including income not reported to you on a Form 1099” (Publication 334).

Set it up once: four pieces

1. A separate account for business money

IRS Publication 583: “One of the first things you should do when you start a business is open a business checking account. You should keep your business account separate from your personal checking account.” As a sole proprietor you don’t need an LLC to do this; see business bank account without an LLC and best business bank accounts. When all client payments land there and business costs go out of it (or one business card), your bank statement becomes most of your record, and moving money to yourself is a simple owner’s draw, not an expense.

2. One place for the records

A spreadsheet or an app; not both, and not “I’ll sort it out in April.” Each row needs the date, who paid or who you paid, the amount, the category, and a note or link to the receipt.

3. Categories that match Schedule C

Freelancers who are sole proprietors (or single-member LLCs) report business profit on Schedule C. If your categories match its lines, tax time is just adding up columns. The ones most freelancers use:

4. A way to capture receipts and miles

Snap a photo when you pay, or forward email receipts to one folder, named by date and vendor. If you drive for work, log the date, destination, purpose and miles for each trip; the 2026 business rate is 72.5¢ a mile for January–June and 76¢ for July–December (IRS). Our rule of thumb: stay with a spreadsheet if you have one business account, fewer than about 40 business transactions a month and little driving, and you actually update it weekly. Move to software when you have several accounts or cards, drive for work, mix business and personal spending on one card, or keep falling behind. Automatic import doesn’t remove the work: you still review each transaction and its category. The routine that keeps it current

WhenWhat to do
Weekly (15 minutes)Categorize new transactions, attach receipts, log any business trips, mark invoices paid.
MonthlyReconcile against your bank and card statements so every transaction is in and nothing is double-counted; look at profit for the month; chase late invoices with our unpaid invoice plan.
QuarterlyTotal year-to-date profit and pay your estimated tax (see the quarterly tax calculator).
JanuaryMatch your totals against the 1099-NEC and 1099-K forms you receive (see 1099-NEC vs 1099-K), send any 1099-NECs you owe to subcontractors, total each Schedule C category, and archive the year.

Pick one accounting method and stick to it. Under the cash method, which most freelancers use, “you report income in the tax year you receive it” and “usually deduct or capitalize expenses in the tax year you pay them” (Publication 583). An invoice sent in December and paid in January is January income.

How long to keep records

From the IRS, counted from when you filed the return (returns filed early count as filed on the due date):

SituationKeep records for
Most returns3 years
You file a claim for credit or refund after filing3 years from filing or 2 years from paying the tax, whichever is later
You leave out income that is more than 25% of the gross income on your return6 years
Bad debt deduction or worthless securities loss7 years
You don’t file, or file a fraudulent returnIndefinitely
Employment tax records (if you have employees)At least 4 years after the tax is due or paid
Property such as equipment you depreciateUntil the period runs out for the year you dispose of it

Keep copies of the filed returns themselves too; the IRS notes they help with future returns and amendments.

Common mistakes

  • Recording deposits instead of invoices paid. Platforms and card processors deduct fees first. Record the gross amount as income and the fee as an expense, or your income won’t match the 1099-K.
  • Counting transfers as income. Moving money from savings or a personal account into the business account isn’t income. Label transfers so they don’t end up in your totals.
  • Treating owner’s draws as expenses. Paying yourself doesn’t reduce a sole proprietor’s profit (why).
  • Reconstructing mileage in April. Estimates aren’t deductible; log trips as they happen.
  • Waiting for 1099s. Clients under the reporting threshold, and most clients abroad, won’t send one. Your own records are the source of truth (see getting paid by international clients).

FAQ

Can I track freelance income and expenses in a spreadsheet?

Yes. The IRS says you may choose any recordkeeping system suited to your business that clearly shows your income and expenses, and that except in a few cases the law does not require any special kind of records. A spreadsheet works if you record every business transaction, keep the receipts and other supporting documents, and can total each category at tax time.

Do I need a separate bank account to track freelance income?

It is not legally required for a sole proprietor, but it makes tracking far easier. IRS Publication 583 says one of the first things you should do when you start a business is open a business checking account and keep it separate from your personal account, and the IRS calls the business checking account the main source for entries in most small businesses' books.

Do I need to keep paper receipts?

Not necessarily. The IRS says all requirements that apply to hard copy books and records also apply to electronic records, and Publication 583 explains when original paper records may be replaced by an electronic storage system. Whatever format you use, the record should show the payee, amount, proof of payment, date and what the expense was for.

How long should I keep my freelance records?

Generally 3 years from the date you filed the return. The IRS lists longer periods: 6 years if you leave out income that is more than 25% of the gross income on your return, 7 years for a bad debt deduction or worthless securities loss, and indefinitely if you do not file or file a fraudulent return. Keep records for property, such as a laptop you depreciate, until the period runs out for the year you dispose of it.

Do I have to report income that wasn't on a 1099?

Yes. IRS Publication 334 says you must report all income you receive from your business, including income not reported to you on a Form 1099. Track every payment as it arrives and use the 1099s you receive only as a cross-check.
