Getting a Car Loan as a Freelancer with Variable Income

Lenders like steady paychecks, and freelance income isn't steady. You can still get an auto loan, and the federal consumer agencies' advice applies to everyone: shop for the loan before the car, compare total cost, and pick a payment you can really afford. This guide covers the steps that matter most when your income moves around. It can't promise approval, because that depends on your credit, income and the lender.

1. Budget for your slowest months

The CFPB says to set a budget before shopping for a loan, and to look at the total cost of financing instead of just the monthly payment. Low payments generally mean a longer loan, and longer loans can mean more interest and more time owing more than the car is worth (negative equity). It also says to budget for taxes, title and dealer fees, plus insurance, fuel, registration and repairs. For a freelancer, a sensible test is whether you could cover the payment in your lowest-earning month in the past year. That test is our suggestion, not an agency rule. (CFPB, Take control of your auto loan) Use the car loan calculator to compare terms.

2. Get preapproved before you visit a dealer

The CFPB and FTC both recommend getting a preapproval or quote from a bank, credit union or other lender before you shop. A preapproval gives you an interest rate, loan length and maximum loan amount, and gives you something to compare with the dealer's offer. The CFPB notes that dealers can charge more than the lender's quoted "buy rate" for arranging financing, which is a reason to negotiate. The CFPB also says shopping for an auto loan within a short window (it cites about 14 to 45 days depending on the scoring model) generally counts as a single inquiry. (FTC, financing or leasing a car)

3. Have your income information ready

The CFPB says lenders generally ask for sources of income, total gross monthly income, current and previous employers and length of employment, occupation, and details on your other debts. The CFPB's list doesn't say which documents prove self-employed income, and each lender decides. Ask what they accept, and gather recent tax returns, 1099s and bank statements so you can answer quickly. Keeping business income in a separate business account and your 1099 forms organized makes this easier.

4. Consider a down payment or co-signer

A larger down payment reduces the amount you borrow and the total cost of the loan. The CFPB also says a co-signer with good credit can significantly lower your interest rate if your credit history is limited or low, but a co-signer is equally responsible for missed payments, so both of you should think carefully. Federal law generally prohibits a lender from requiring a co-signer if you apply individually and meet the lender's creditworthiness standards.

5. Negotiate the whole deal, not only the payment

The FTC advises getting an "out-the-door" price in writing, including taxes and fees, before discussing financing, and negotiating the APR and terms just as you would the price. The CFPB lists negotiable items: trade-in value, down payment, APR, loan length, prepayment penalty, add-ons such as extended warranties and GAP insurance, and dealer fees. Taxes, title and registration fees are set by government and aren't negotiable. If you buy add-ons, you can generally cancel them later, per the CFPB, which could save money.

6. Be careful with "no credit, no problem" offers

The CFPB says "buy here, pay here" dealerships that finance in-house are likely to charge higher interest rates than banks and credit unions, and that lenders to people with no or poor credit may be allowed to use devices that help repossess or disable the vehicle if you miss a payment. Even with poor or no credit, it suggests getting a quote from a bank or credit union first.

7. Check the paperwork before you sign

Lenders must give you written disclosures under the Truth in Lending Act, including APR, finance charge, amount financed and total of payments. The CFPB advises making sure the loan paperwork is finalized before you sign the purchase contract, since a dealer could ask you to return and sign different terms (a "spot delivery" risk). If you aren't comfortable, you can walk away.

FAQ

Can I get a car loan with irregular income?

Many lenders consider self-employed applicants, but each sets its own requirements. Getting quotes from several banks, credit unions and online lenders shows which will work with your documentation.

Does shopping around hurt my credit?

According to the CFPB, shopping for an auto loan generally has little to no impact, especially when done within a short period, though it depends on the scoring model.

Is a longer loan a good idea to lower the payment?

It lowers the payment but raises total interest and keeps you at risk of negative equity longer, per the CFPB and FTC.
