W-2 Job Plus Side Business: Extra W-4 Withholding vs Quarterly Estimated Taxes

By Freelance Ledger · Published November 26, 2026 · How we research

Rules checked on November 26, 2026 against the IRS 2026 Form W-4 and its instructions, the Tax Withholding Estimator page, Publication 505 (2026), the estimated taxes page (reviewed Nov. 23, 2026), the 2026 Form 1040-ES and the Form 2210 instructions. Federal rules only; states have their own. General information, not tax advice.

If you have a regular job and freelance on the side, nobody withholds tax from the side money. The usual advice is "make quarterly estimated payments." There's a second option the IRS spells out itself: have your employer withhold extra from your paycheck to cover it. This page compares the two, shows how to size the extra withholding with 2026 numbers, and covers the one rule that makes withholding unusually forgiving.

The short version

  • Both work. The IRS estimated taxes page: "If you receive salaries and wages, you can avoid having to pay estimated tax by asking your employer to withhold more tax from your earnings."
  • Where on the W-4: Step 4(c), "Extra withholding," a flat dollar amount per paycheck. Don't put freelance income in Step 4(a); the form says not to include self-employment income there.
  • Withholding covers self-employment tax too. It's all one tax bill on your Form 1040.
  • The big advantage: withheld tax is treated as paid evenly through the year for the penalty, even if most of it came out in November and December. Estimated payments count only on the day you pay.
  • Example: single, $70,000 salary, $20,000 side profit → about $6,097.23 of extra federal tax for 2026, or $234.51 more per biweekly paycheck.

Why withholding is so forgiving

The underpayment penalty is figured quarter by quarter. For estimated payments, each one counts on the day you make it. Withholding is different. The Form 2210 instructions say that for withheld federal income tax "you are considered to have paid one-fourth of these amounts on each payment due date unless you can show otherwise." (Form 2210 lets you elect to use the actual dates instead, if that gives you a lower penalty.)

So if you realize in October that you haven't paid anything on your side income, a large Step 4(c) amount on your last few paychecks is treated as if it had been paid evenly since spring. An estimated payment made in October can't do that for the April, June and September dates. Our missed-payment penalty guide shows what those earlier quarters can cost.

How to set it up, step by step

  1. Estimate the extra tax from the side business. That's self-employment tax plus income tax on the profit at your bracket. Our set-aside calculator and self-employment tax calculator do the math; the example below shows it by hand.
  2. Or let the IRS estimator do it. The 2026 Form W-4 says that if you want to pay tax on self-employment income through withholding, "use the estimator at www.irs.gov/W4App to figure the amount to have withheld." The Tax Withholding Estimator asks for recent pay stubs and "payment records for self-employment, gig work," takes about 25 minutes, and can produce a pre-filled W-4. It only works if you have a W-2 job or a pension with withholding.
  3. Divide by the paychecks left this year and enter the result in Step 4(c) of a new Form W-4. If you're married filing jointly, either spouse's W-4 can carry it. Publication 505's own example splits a $4,459 shortfall over 49 remaining paydays and puts $91 on the spouse's W-4.
  4. Give it to payroll. Per Publication 505, a current-year change must take effect no later than the start of the first payroll period ending on or after the 30th day after you turn it in.
  5. Check your next pay stub to confirm the higher federal withholding.
  6. Recheck in late December and every January. The IRS estimator page says that if you change withholding during the year, "you may need to update it again in late December to withhold the right amount next year." That matters most after a big catch-up amount.

Example 2: catching up late in the year

Same person, but they made no estimated payments and notice in the fall with five biweekly paychecks left. Putting $1,219.45 in Step 4(c) ($6,097.23 ÷ 5) covers the side-business tax, and because withholding is treated as paid evenly, the April, June and September due dates count as covered too. Then they file a new W-4 in late December to drop the amount back down (to about $234.51 if the side income continues in 2027). Make sure the catch-up paychecks can absorb that much; a big Step 4(c) amount can take a large bite out of take-home pay.

Example 3: aiming for the prior-year safe harbor

You don't have to predict this year's side income exactly. The penalty generally doesn't apply if withholding and timely payments cover 100% of last year's total tax (110% if last year's AGI was over $150,000, or $75,000 married filing separately), per the 2026 Form 1040-ES. Say last year's total tax was $9,200 and the job is on track to withhold $6,570 this year. The gap is $2,630, or $101.15 extra per biweekly paycheck across a full year. If the side business grows, you'll owe a balance when you file, but no underpayment penalty.

When quarterly estimated payments make more sense

  • Your side income is lumpy or uncertain. Estimates can be sized each quarter. And if most income arrives late in the year, the annualized income installment method (Form 2210, Schedule AI) can lower or remove the penalty. Fixed withholding can't adapt.
  • You might leave the job. The withholding plan ends with the paycheck.
  • Cash flow is tight. Withholding takes the money every payday; estimates let you hold it until each due date.
  • You'd rather your employer not see a large Step 4(c) number. It's only a dollar amount, but some people prefer to keep it separate.

You can also mix them: withholding for the baseline, plus an estimated payment after an unusually big project. Estimated payment dates are on our 2027 due dates page, and how to pay online covers Direct Pay and the IRS Online Account now that EFTPS is closed to individuals.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Putting side-gig profit in Step 4(a). The W-4 instructions exclude self-employment income from 4(a). Use 4(c) or the estimator.
  • Forgetting self-employment tax. 15.3% on 92.35% of profit adds up fast, and it's on top of income tax. In Example 1 it's $2,825.91 of the $6,097.23.
  • Not undoing a catch-up amount. A December-sized Step 4(c) left in place for next year over-withholds all year.
  • Assuming a refund means you're fine. The penalty is quarterly; you can get a refund and still owe one if earlier quarters were underpaid by estimates (though not by evenly treated withholding).
  • Ignoring state tax. Federal withholding doesn't cover your state.

FAQ

Can I have my employer withhold extra to cover my 1099 side income?

Yes. The IRS estimated taxes page says that if you get wages you can avoid estimated tax by asking your employer to withhold more, using the special line on Form W-4 for an additional amount. On the 2026 Form W-4 that's Step 4(c), a flat dollar amount taken from every paycheck. Federal income tax withholding counts toward your whole tax bill, including self-employment tax.

Should I put my freelance income in Step 4(a) of Form W-4?

No. The 2026 Form W-4 instructions say Step 4(a) is for other income such as interest, dividends and retirement income, and that you shouldn't include income from any jobs or self-employment there. For self-employment income, the form points you to the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator, which can give you the Step 4(c) amount.

Is extra withholding better than quarterly estimated payments?

It's often easier, and it has one real advantage: for the underpayment penalty, the IRS treats income tax withheld as paid in equal parts on the four due dates unless you show otherwise, while an estimated payment only counts on the day you make it. So withholding added late in the year can still cover earlier quarters. Estimated payments are more flexible when side income is uneven or might stop.

How long does a new W-4 take to kick in?

Publication 505 says that for a change in the current year, your employer must put the new Form W-4 into effect no later than the start of the first payroll period ending on or after the 30th day after you hand it in. Many payroll systems are faster, but plan for up to a month.

Do I still need to make estimated payments if my withholding covers the side gig?

Generally not. The IRS says you can avoid estimated tax by having more withheld, and you're safe from the penalty if you owe less than $1,000 after withholding and credits, or your withholding and timely payments cover at least 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's (110% if last year's AGI was over $150,000, $75,000 married filing separately). You may still owe a balance when you file; that isn't a penalty.

What about state taxes?

This page covers federal tax only. Most states with an income tax have their own withholding certificate and estimated-tax rules, so check your state revenue department if you want state tax on your side income withheld too.
