How to Pay Estimated Taxes Online Now That EFTPS Is Closed to Individuals

By Freelance Ledger · Published November 25, 2026 · How we research

Checked on November 25, 2026 against the IRS payments page, Direct Pay and its help page and payment-types tip sheet, the Online Account page and FAQ, the IRS EFTPS page and EFTPS.gov, the IRS card payments page and card frequency limits, the estimated taxes page and the 2026 Form 1040-ES. Federal payments only; states have their own. General information, not tax advice.

If you paid your quarterly taxes through EFTPS, the system you used is gone for individuals. In mid-November 2026 the IRS stopped taking individual payments through EFTPS and pointed everyone to two free options: your IRS Online Account and IRS Direct Pay. With the last 2026 estimated payment due Friday, January 15, 2027, here's how each option works, what cards cost, and the details that trip people up.

The short version

  • Free: pay from a U.S. bank account in your IRS Online Account (sign-in, payment history, scheduling) or with IRS Direct Pay (no sign-in).
  • EFTPS: closed to individual payments since Nov. 12–13, 2026. Enrolled users can still sign in to view history and cancel old scheduled payments.
  • Card: about 1.75%–1.85% for credit cards, about $2 flat for debit. Limited to 2 card payments per quarter for estimated tax.
  • Pick the right year: the January 15, 2027 payment is 2026 estimated tax.
  • Keep proof: save the confirmation number and check your Online Account a couple of business days later.

What changed with EFTPS

The IRS EFTPS page now says individual taxpayers can no longer create new EFTPS accounts, and "as of Nov. 12, 2026, EFTPS is no longer available for individuals to make payments." The notice on EFTPS.gov gives the date as November 13, 2026 and says individuals "can no longer make or modify payments" there. Either way, it's done.

  • If you already had EFTPS: you can still sign in to see your payment history, and the IRS says you can cancel a previously scheduled payment up to two business days before its date. Neither notice says what happens to old scheduled payments you leave alone, so if you had any set up for January, sign in to your IRS Online Account and confirm what's actually scheduled before you rely on it.
  • Who it affects: individuals paying their own tax, which includes 1040-ES estimated tax for sole proprietors and single-member LLC owners who file Schedule C. EFTPS.gov describes itself as a service "for businesses," and the IRS says some business tax payments must still go through EFTPS, so an LLC or S corp paying business taxes under its EIN isn't covered by this change.
  • Paper checks still work for now. The IRS payments modernization page says that until further notice, existing forms and procedures should be used to make payments, though it asks people to pay electronically to avoid delays.

Step by step: IRS Online Account

  1. Go to IRS.gov/account and sign in or create an account. The IRS says new users should have photo ID ready for identity verification.
  2. Choose to make a payment from your bank account. Select estimated tax as the payment type and 2026 as the tax year for the January 15, 2027 installment.
  3. Enter your U.S. checking or savings account (you can save it to your profile) and the date. You can pay the same day or schedule up to 365 days ahead, so you can set up all four 2027 payments at once if you know the amounts.
  4. Opt in to the email confirmation. Your payment activity page then lists pending, scheduled, processed, canceled and returned payments, and the IRS says you can see up to 5 years of history, including estimated tax payments. That's the number you'll want at filing time.

Step by step: IRS Direct Pay (no account)

  1. Open Direct Pay and choose Make a payment.
  2. Reason for payment: Estimated Tax. Apply payment to: 1040ES. Tax year: 2026 for the January 15, 2027 payment. The IRS tip sheet says estimated payments can go to the current or previous year in January, and only the current year from February to December.
  3. Verify your identity with information from a past return. It can't be the year you're paying for, so pick an older filed return and enter your name and address exactly as they appear on it. The IRS says you can't use Direct Pay if you've never filed a federal return or you're paying on your first one.
  4. Enter your bank account and payment date (same day or up to 365 days ahead), review and sign.
  5. Save the confirmation number or take the email option. The IRS says Direct Pay can't retrieve your confirmation number after you leave, and you need it to look up, change or cancel the payment (up to two business days before the payment date).

Direct Pay only schedules one payment at a time and asks you to verify your identity each time you come back. If you pay monthly, the Online Account is less work.

  • Digital wallets: Pay1040 takes PayPal and Click to Pay; ACI takes PayPal, Venmo and Click to Pay.
  • A 1.5% cash-back card earns $30.00 on a $2,000.00 payment that costs $35.00 in fees. Card payments rarely pay off unless you're working toward a sign-up bonus.
  • The IRS limits card and cash payments to 2 per quarter for Form 1040-ES (frequency limit table).
  • To cancel a card payment you contact the processor, not the IRS. The IRS page says card processing fees "are tax deductible for businesses"; whether a fee on your personal estimated tax qualifies is a question for your tax preparer.

Timing: when a payment counts as on time

  • The IRS says Direct Pay and Online Account payments made for the due date count as on time even if the money leaves your account later. Payments over $1 million and payments made on weekends, bank holidays or after 3 p.m. Eastern on a business day may be withdrawn the next business day.
  • Both systems are down from 11:45 p.m. to midnight Eastern every night. Don't leave it to the last 15 minutes.
  • If a due date falls on a weekend or legal holiday, the 2026 Form 1040-ES says to use the next business day. January 15, 2027 is a Friday, so no shift this time.
  • A confirmation number only means the IRS will try to pull the money. If your bank account is short, the payment fails, and the IRS says you'll get a notice asking you to pay again. Check your Online Account about two business days after the payment date.

Paying by mail instead

The 2026 Form 1040-ES has a separate voucher for each due date. Make the check payable to "United States Treasury," write "2026 Form 1040-ES" and your SSN on it, put only the check amount on the voucher, and enclose it without staples. Mail it to the address for your state in the form's instructions. The IRS check page says to allow 2 weeks for processing, and the money may be pulled electronically from your account the same day it arrives.

Paying more often than quarterly

The IRS estimated taxes page says you can pay weekly, every two weeks or monthly "as long as you've paid enough in by the end of the quarter," and the Direct Pay tip sheet says you don't have to tag each payment with a month or quarter. A freelancer with an $8,000.00 federal estimate for the year can pay $2,000.00 on each due date or send money after every client payment. Our tax set-aside calculator gives you a percentage to move each time. Just make sure the total by each due date covers that installment, because the underpayment penalty is figured due date by due date (the IRS rate rises to 8% on January 1, 2027).

Mistakes to avoid

  • Wrong tax year in January. A January 15, 2027 payment marked 2027 counts toward next year, not the installment you owe.
  • Picking "balance due" instead of "estimated tax." Choose estimated tax (1040ES) so it's treated as an estimated payment for the year.
  • Counting on old EFTPS schedules. Confirm in your Online Account what's actually scheduled.
  • Not saving confirmations. Keep the number and a screenshot with your tax records. You'll enter the year's total estimated payments on your return.
  • Married couples: the IRS says either spouse can make estimated payments, but they show only under the spouse who paid until a joint return is filed. Don't panic if your spouse's account looks empty.
  • Forgetting your state. These are federal payments only. States with an income tax run their own estimated payment systems.

FAQ

Can I still use EFTPS to pay my estimated taxes?

Not as an individual. The IRS says individual taxpayers can no longer create new EFTPS accounts and that, as of Nov. 12, 2026, EFTPS is no longer available for individuals to make payments (EFTPS.gov's own notice says Nov. 13). If you're enrolled you can still sign in to see your payment history and cancel a payment you scheduled earlier. Use your IRS Online Account or IRS Direct Pay instead. Businesses paying business taxes can still use EFTPS.

What's the free way to pay quarterly estimated taxes online?

Pay from a U.S. checking or savings account through your IRS Online Account (sign-in required) or IRS Direct Pay (no sign-in). Both are free. Card and digital-wallet payments go through IRS-approved processors that charge a fee.

Which tax year do I pick for the January 15, 2027 payment?

2026. The fourth installment of 2026 estimated tax is due January 15, 2027, so it belongs to tax year 2026. The IRS Direct Pay tip sheet says estimated tax payments can be applied to the current or previous year in January, and only the current year from February to December.

Is there a fee to pay estimated taxes with a credit card?

Yes. As of Nov. 25, 2026 the IRS card page lists Pay1040 at 1.75% for credit cards ($2.50 minimum) or $2.15 for debit, and ACI Payments at 1.85% ($2.50 minimum) or $2.10 for debit. On a $2,000 payment that's $35.00 or $37.00 by credit card. The IRS limits card payments for estimated tax to 2 per quarter.

How do I know the IRS got my payment?

Save the confirmation number. Then check the payment activity in your IRS Online Account: the IRS says bank payments made through the Online Account or Direct Pay show up there immediately, card payments 1 to 2 days after the payment date, and checks can take up to three weeks. Direct Pay's help page suggests checking about two business days after the payment date to confirm it went through.

Can I pay estimated taxes monthly instead of quarterly?

Yes. The IRS estimated taxes page says you can pay weekly, every two weeks or monthly, as long as you've paid enough by the end of each quarter. You don't have to label each payment with a quarter.
