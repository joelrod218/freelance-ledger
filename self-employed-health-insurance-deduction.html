Self-Employed Health Insurance Deduction: Who Qualifies, What Counts and the Profit Limit

By Freelance Ledger · Published November 24, 2026 · How we research

Rules checked on November 24, 2026 against the IRS 2026 Form 7206, the Instructions for Form 7206 (2025, the latest posted; the IRS Form 7206 page lists no recent developments), Publication 334 (2025), Publication 974 (2025), Publication 502 and the Form 8995 instructions. Federal rules only; states have their own. General information, not tax advice.

If you buy your own health insurance as a freelancer, the premiums can be one of your biggest write-offs. It isn't a business expense on Schedule C, though. It's a separate deduction on Schedule 1 with its own rules: a month-by-month test for employer coverage and a cap tied to your profit. This page walks through who qualifies, which premiums count and how the limit works, with dollar examples.

The short version

  • Who: self-employed people with a net profit (Schedule C or F), partners with self-employment earnings, and more-than-2% S corporation shareholders whose premiums are reported as W-2 wages.
  • What counts: medical, dental and vision premiums for you, your spouse, your dependents and any child under 27 at year-end, plus qualified long-term care premiums up to an age-based cap and Medicare premiums you pay voluntarily.
  • Two limits: no deduction for any month you were eligible for a subsidized employer plan (yours or your spouse's), and the deduction can't exceed your profit from the business after half of self-employment tax and retirement-plan contributions.
  • Where: Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 17. It lowers income tax, not self-employment tax.
  • Example: $60,000 of profit and $600 a month in premiums all year → a $7,200 deduction, worth about $1,584 of federal income tax at a 22% marginal rate.

Who qualifies

The Form 7206 instructions say one of these must be true: you were self-employed with a net profit on Schedule C or Schedule F, you were a partner with net earnings from self-employment (Schedule K-1, box 14, code A), you used an optional method to figure net earnings on Schedule SE, or you got wages from an S corporation in which you were a more-than-2% shareholder.

The plan also has to be established under your business. For a Schedule C freelancer that's easy: the IRS says the policy can be in the business's name or in your own name. A Marketplace plan or a private policy you bought yourself works. (Partners and S corp owners have extra steps; see below.)

The employer-plan test (month by month)

You can't count premiums for any month you were eligible to participate in a health plan subsidized by your employer, your spouse's employer, or the employer of your dependent or of your child under 27. Eligibility is what matters, not enrollment. The instructions say this applies "even if you didn't actually participate."

  • A W-2 day job that offers subsidized coverage you turned down blocks the deduction for those months.
  • A spouse whose employer offers subsidized family coverage blocks it too.
  • Because it's monthly, a mid-year change (you quit the job, your spouse's coverage ends) opens up the remaining months.
  • The rule is applied separately to long-term care plans and to other health plans.

The profit limit

The deduction can't be more than the earned income from the business the plan is established under. Form 7206 gets there in a few lines:

  1. Start with net profit from that business (Schedule C line 31).
  2. Subtract the deductible half of self-employment tax (Schedule 1, line 15), shared out by profit if you have more than one business.
  3. Subtract SEP, SIMPLE or solo 401(k) contributions for yourself from that business (Schedule 1, line 16).
  4. Your deduction is the smaller of your eligible premiums and that result.

For most full-time freelancers the cap never bites. It matters in a first year, a slow year, or when a side business earns less than the family's premiums. For example, $30,000 of profit with a $5,000 SEP contribution caps the deduction at $22,880.56 ($30,000 − $2,119.44 half of SE tax − $5,000).

What the deduction does and doesn't do

  • Lowers AGI. It's an "above the line" adjustment on Schedule 1, so you get it whether or not you itemize.
  • Doesn't lower self-employment tax. The Form 7206 instructions say you can't subtract it when figuring net earnings from self-employment for the business the plan is under.
  • Can't be doubled up. Don't include the deducted amount in any Schedule A medical deduction. Only premiums you couldn't deduct on line 17 can go there, and Publication 502 says medical expenses on Schedule A only count above 7.5% of AGI.
  • Can trim the QBI deduction. The Form 8995 instructions list the self-employed health insurance deduction among business-related items that reduce qualified business income. Tax software usually handles this automatically.

Marketplace plans and the premium tax credit

If you buy coverage through the Health Insurance Marketplace and get advance payments of the premium tax credit, the two benefits affect each other: the deduction lowers your income, which can raise the credit, and the credit lowers the premium you can deduct. Publication 334 and the Form 7206 instructions send you to Publication 974, which has worksheets with a shorter "simplified" method and an "iterative" method that can give a better result. Good tax software runs this for you. If you file by hand, budget time for it, and reconcile the credit on Form 8962 either way. From 2026 there's no cap on repaying excess advance credit and no credit above 400% of the poverty line; see Marketplace health insurance for freelancers for how to estimate income and avoid a big repayment.

S corp owners and partners

  • More-than-2% S corp shareholders: the policy can be in your name or the corporation's, but the S corporation must pay or reimburse the premiums and report them as wages in box 1 of your W-2. Otherwise the plan isn't considered established under the business. The limit then uses your Medicare wages from that S corp (Form 7206 line 11). If you're weighing the election, see our S corp vs sole proprietor calculator.
  • Partners: the partnership pays or reimburses the premiums and reports them as guaranteed payments on your Schedule K-1.

How to claim it

The deduction goes on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 17. The Form 7206 instructions say you can generally use the worksheet in the Form 1040 instructions, but you must use Form 7206 if any of these apply:

  • You had more than one source of income subject to self-employment tax.
  • You file Form 2555 (foreign earned income).
  • You're including qualified long-term care premiums.

Two or more plans under different businesses need a separate Form 7206 for each.

Records to keep

  • Your premium statements or the Marketplace's Form 1095-A, showing what you paid each month.
  • Proof of which months you (or your spouse) were and weren't eligible for an employer plan, such as an offer letter, an HR coverage notice or termination dates.
  • Pay premiums from a single account so the year's total is easy to add up. Our guide to tracking income and expenses has a simple routine.

FAQ

Can I deduct health insurance as a 1099 contractor?

Often, yes. If you're self-employed with a net profit on Schedule C, the IRS lets you deduct premiums you paid for medical, dental and vision insurance (and, within age limits, qualified long-term care insurance) for yourself, your spouse and your dependents as an adjustment to income on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 17. Two limits apply: you can't count any month you were eligible for a subsidized plan through your own or your spouse's employer, and the deduction can't be more than your net profit from the business after the deductible half of self-employment tax and any SEP, SIMPLE or solo 401(k) contributions for that business.

Does the self-employed health insurance deduction reduce self-employment tax?

No. The Form 7206 instructions say you can't subtract it when figuring net earnings for self-employment tax from the business the plan is established under. It reduces your adjusted gross income and therefore federal income tax, but your self-employment tax (15.3% on 92.35% of profit up to the Social Security base) stays the same.

Can I take the deduction if my spouse has insurance through work?

Not for any month you were eligible to join your spouse's employer-subsidized plan, even if you chose not to enroll. The test is month by month, so if that eligibility ended partway through the year (for example your spouse changed jobs), premiums for the months after it ended can still count.

Do Marketplace (ACA) premiums qualify?

They can. For a sole proprietor, the policy can be in your own name and still count as established under your business. But only the part you actually pay counts: if advance premium tax credit lowered your premium, the deduction and the credit affect each other, and IRS Publication 974 has worksheets (a simplified method and an iterative method) to figure both.

Can I deduct Medicare premiums as a self-employed person?

Yes, if you have self-employment profit. The Form 7206 instructions say Medicare premiums you voluntarily pay to obtain insurance in your name that is similar to qualifying private health insurance can be used to figure the deduction. The same profit limit and employer-plan rule apply.

What if my premiums are more than my profit?

The deduction stops at the profit limit. The IRS says premiums you can't deduct on Schedule 1 can be included as medical expenses on Schedule A if you itemize, where total medical expenses only count above 7.5% of your AGI. You can't count the same dollars in both places.
